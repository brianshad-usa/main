Evaluation Criteria

6 things to look for in an LA managed IT company

Six criteria that separate one proposal from another.

Local Presence and Response Time

A provider with engineers in the LA metro can come on-site when remote support is not enough, attend business reviews in person, and be held accountable over the long term. Ask any prospective provider where its engineers are based and what its contractual on-site response time is, not only the average. For a closer look at local managed IT, visit our managed IT services page.

Flat-Rate vs. Per-Incident Pricing

The pricing model shows where a provider's incentives lie. Per-incident or per-hour billing can reward slow resolution. Flat-rate, all-inclusive pricing rewards the provider for keeping systems running. Always ask what is included in the flat rate and what might generate extra charges. For price ranges and what each service tier should include, see our 2026 LA managed IT pricing guide.

Security and Industry Fit

Los Angeles is home to a wide range of businesses with strict data-handling obligations — healthcare clinics, financial and retail firms, defense manufacturers, and law firms among them. If your business is regulated, confirm that any provider you consider is familiar with your industry's requirements and can explain exactly which security controls they manage: MFA, encryption, patching, monitoring, and backups verified by restore testing rather than assumed. See how we approach cybersecurity.

Proactive vs. Reactive Support

There is a meaningful difference between a provider that fixes problems after they happen and one that prevents them. Proactive providers use monitoring, automated patching, vulnerability scanning and regular health checks to catch issues before they affect the business. Ask what share of a provider's work is reactive tickets and what share is preventive. A provider that measures it will have data to show.

No Long-Term Contract Requirement

A multi-year contract before you have experienced the service asks you to accept a promise. Ask what the minimum term is, what happens if service falls short, and how the agreement ends. Client retention is a better sign than contract length.

Verifiable Client References

A provider should be able to connect you with current clients in industries similar to yours. References tell you what the relationship looks like after the sale: response during a real outage, how onboarding went, how the provider communicates during incidents, and whether it delivers what was promised. Ask for two or three and call them.

Warning Signs

Red flags to watch out for

These are the warning signs that should prompt you to ask harder questions — or walk away entirely.

  • Offshore help desk with slow escalation. If first-line support reads from a script in another country, your team will spend more time explaining a problem than resolving it. Ask where the people who answer your calls are based and what their technical qualifications are.
  • Per-hour or per-incident billing for ongoing support. The longer a problem takes to fix, the more the provider earns. For day-to-day support, flat-rate pricing is usually the better fit.
  • No SLA or vague response-time commitments. "We respond quickly" is not a service level agreement. A reputable provider will specify response times in writing, broken down by severity level, and be willing to back those commitments with credits or remedies if they are not met.
  • Requires a 2- to 3-year contract upfront. Be cautious of a multi-year commitment before you have any experience of the team or the service.
  • Cannot provide client references in your industry. Environments and security needs differ by sector. A provider should be able to point to clients in a similar one.
Due Diligence

Questions to ask before signing with an IT provider

Bring this list to every sales conversation. A provider who hesitates or gives vague answers to any of these questions is telling you something important.

  1. What is your average and contractual response time for remote support requests? Ask for both the average and the SLA commitment, and request a sample SLA document.
  2. Who answers the phone at 2 AM? Is it a local engineer, an on-call rotation of technicians, or an offshore call center? What is their level of technical authority?
  3. If we are regulated, how do you handle the IT side of our requirements? Ask which security controls they manage (MFA, encryption, logging, patching, backups), how they document that work, and how they coordinate with your compliance advisor or auditor.
  4. How do you handle onboarding and the transition from our current provider? What is the process for credential transfer, documentation, and avoiding a gap in support?
  5. What is included in the flat monthly rate, and what costs extra? Get a full list of inclusions and exclusions in writing before signing anything.
  6. Where is your team based? Are the engineers who will support my business local, remote, or a mix? What is your on-site coverage area and response time?
  7. Do you require a long-term contract? If so, why? What happens if service quality falls below the promised standard?
  8. What is your client retention rate? How long does the average client stay with you? What is your churn rate over the past three years?
  9. Can you provide two or three references in my industry? Ask for clients who are similar in size and sector and actually follow up.
  10. How do you handle after-hours emergencies? Is there a dedicated emergency line? What is the escalation path and how quickly can a senior engineer be reached?
The Local Advantage

Why local matters in Los Angeles

Los Angeles combines industries with very different requirements. Entertainment studios in Burbank and Culver City run production systems with specialized security needs. Healthcare groups across the San Fernando Valley and West LA protect patient records. Aerospace and defense suppliers in El Segundo and Torrance safeguard controlled technical data. Legal and financial firms in Century City and Downtown LA have their own data governance rules.

A provider that works in the region knows the connectivity options in Chatsworth and Century City, the staffing patterns in healthcare and entertainment, and the obligations a Pasadena biotech and a Sherman Oaks law firm each carry.

Local also means accountability. Engineers who work in the same region can come on-site when needed and know your team by name, and when something goes wrong you know whom to call. Pro Link Systems has worked this way with Los Angeles organizations since 1999.
