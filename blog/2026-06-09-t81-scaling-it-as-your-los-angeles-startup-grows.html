Scaling IT as Your LA Startup Grows

Shared logins and one-person knowledge break first as a startup grows. Tests you can run now, what to formalize at each stage, and whether to outsource, hire or share the work.

Scaling IT in a Los Angeles startup: where it breaks first

Scaling IT for a Los Angeles startup is mostly about replacing habits that worked for five people before they cause damage at twenty-five: shared logins, laptops set up by whoever was free, and one engineer who knows how everything is wired. This article is for founders, chief operating officers and heads of finance or operations who are deciding when to formalize IT, who should run it, and what investors and enterprise customers will expect to see. The headcount bands below are a rough guide drawn from common practice, not a standard; the point at which a company needs each step depends on the data it holds and who it sells to.

Signs the current setup has run out

Each sign has a test you can run this week, which is more useful than a feeling that IT is slow.

  • New hires are not productive on day one. Time how long it takes from signed offer to a working laptop with the right access. If it takes days, setup depends on a person rather than a process.
  • Nobody can say who has access to what. Pick a departing employee from the last year and check how long their access took to remove, and from how many systems.
  • Engineers or operations staff spend regular hours on IT tickets. That time is a cost even though no invoice shows it.
  • A customer or investor sent a security questionnaire and you could not answer it. Expect more, and ask which items you could not evidence.
  • Nobody has restored from a backup. A backup that has never been restored is an assumption.

What to put in place at each stage

A staged approach (rough headcount bands)
StagePut in placeDecision to make
Up to about 10 peopleCompany-owned accounts for every tool, multi-factor authentication on all of them, a password manager, cloud storage rather than local files, and a backup of anything that is not in a cloud serviceWho owns the admin accounts? Make it the company, not an individual
About 10 to 50 peopleStandard laptop setup, a written onboarding and offboarding checklist, a single place for support requests (a help desk), documented systems and a tested recoveryKeep IT as a side job, hire a first IT person, or outsource? See below
50 people and aboveEndpoint detection and response, network and access segmentation, regular access reviews, security awareness training, central logging, and a vendor review processWhether to pursue an independent attestation such as SOC 2 because customers ask for it

NIST publishes small-business cybersecurity resources, including guidance for using its Cybersecurity Framework at small scale; they give a reasonable checklist for the first two stages. For multi-factor authentication, the CISA ransomware guide recommends phishing-resistant methods for email, remote access and accounts that reach critical systems. On attestation: a SOC 2 report is issued by a certified public accountant who examines controls at a service organization (AICPA). It is a business decision driven by what your customers require, and an auditor or counsel should advise you on scope; an IT provider cannot give you the report.

Outsource, hire or share the work

There are three workable models, and the right one changes as the company grows.

  • Outsourced managed IT services. Suits a company that wants a help desk, device management and security baselines without hiring. The trade-off is less day-to-day familiarity with your product and engineering environment, so decide what stays with engineering.
  • A first in-house hire. Brings someone who knows the business, but covers one person's skills, holidays and sickness. Plan for who covers when that person is away.
  • Co-managed. An in-house person owns priorities and engineering-adjacent systems while a provider covers the help desk, monitoring and after-hours work. It can suit a company around the 50-person stage.

Whichever you choose, compare total cost, not the monthly fee alone: add the engineer time you recover, the cost of an outage on your busiest day, and the hours you spend answering customer questionnaires. Do not trust a budget rule of thumb expressed as a share of revenue unless you can see where it came from; build the budget from the systems and risks you have.

How to judge whether IT has scaled

Decision for leadership: every quarter, ask for four numbers: days from offer to productive laptop, time to remove a leaver's access, date of the last successful restore, and the share of accounts with multi-factor authentication. If any of them is unknown, that is the next thing to fix.

References

  • Small Business Cybersecurity Corner, NIST.
  • #StopRansomware Guide, Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency.
  • System and Organization Controls (SOC) Suite of Services, AICPA.
