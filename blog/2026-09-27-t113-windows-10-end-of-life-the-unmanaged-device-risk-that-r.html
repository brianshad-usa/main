Windows 10 Is Gone. The Devices Aren't.

Windows 10 support ended in October 2025. What remains is the machines no inventory lists, and the update coverage that lapses without a symptom.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 27, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The refresh list was built from the systems that already knew
  2. Several products share the name Windows 10
  3. Extended Security Updates fail without a symptom
  4. What gets stolen from an old PC is often a session
  5. Some machines genuinely cannot move
  6. Three answers to have in writing before the next renewal

Ask an IT director whether the Windows 10 migration is finished, and the answer comes quickly. Ask how many Windows devices touch company data, and the answer slows down. The distance between those two responses is the entire subject.

Microsoft ended support for Windows 10 on October 14, 2025, a date the company published years in advance. Many organizations handled it the way they handle any dated obligation: as a project. Budget approved, machines counted, an upgrade wave scheduled, a completion memo circulated, attention moved on. Projects have the convenient property of ending. Exposure does not. This essay is for the executives and IT leaders who signed that memo. The decision it informs is whether to keep hunting for forgotten machines or to change what a forgotten machine can open.

The refresh list was built from the systems that already knew

Every upgrade plan starts from a source. Usually it is the asset inventory inside the management platform, or a depreciation schedule from finance, or both stapled together. Those two lists often disagree, and neither of them describes the real device population.

What goes missing is not exotic. A PC embedded inside a piece of equipment, sold as part of the equipment and never thought of as a computer. The machine running the lobby display or the conference room scheduler. A workstation at a second site that a department opened without involving IT. A founder's personal laptop with a saved mail profile. A contractor's machine carrying a VPN certificate issued three years ago and never revoked. A warehouse kiosk. A workstation that talks to a legacy vertical application nobody wants to touch.

None of these appeared on the upgrade list, because the upgrade list was assembled from the systems that already knew about them. That circularity is the heart of the problem: an inventory built from managed devices cannot describe unmanaged ones. The only way to find them is to observe what is actually on the network and authenticating to your services, then reconcile that against what you believe you own. Reconciliation is a standing discipline rather than a deliverable.

Several products share the name Windows 10

Before anything on the reconciled list is condemned or excused, read its edition. The dates differ by years, and the PCs built into equipment are the kind Microsoft's long-term editions were made for.

Extended Security Updates fail without a symptom

For organizations that could not finish in time, Microsoft sells Extended Security Updates for Windows 10 version 22H2. The published price is $61 per device for the first year, doubling each year, for at most three years, and the program is cumulative, so a device that joins in Year Two pays for Year One as well. It was the right call for many businesses and bought genuine time. It also changed the shape of the risk rather than removing it.

Two properties deserve executive attention. The program delivers critical and important security updates only, so nothing about reliability or forward compatibility improves while it runs. And it is bought and activated by device and by year, which means it has to be tracked as an asset-level subscription rather than as a single line in a software budget. Personal computers can be enrolled in a separate consumer program that your management tools may never see.

The failure mode is what gets missed. When an entitlement lapses (a renewal skipped, a device rebuilt and never re-enrolled, a purchase order that went unsigned) nothing visible has to happen. The machine boots. Users log in. Work continues. Patches stop arriving, the gap widens every month, and nothing surfaces until something exploits it. Year One is the first test: a device activated only for that year has had no coverage since October 13, 2026. This next point is analysis rather than established fact: organizations that lose coverage will mostly lose it administratively rather than deliberately, and may not notice for months.

What gets stolen from an old PC is often a session

Executives still picture the risk of an outdated computer as something that happens to that computer. Ransomware encrypts it. Malware slows it down. It gets replaced.

The pattern that matters now is quieter. An unpatched, unmanaged endpoint is an inexpensive foothold, and what an intruder takes from it may not be files at all. It is credentials and live session tokens sitting in the browser. A stolen token can be replayed from somewhere else, and because the sign-in requirements were already met, multifactor authentication included, access is granted. The compromised machine may never do anything alarming again.

This is an identity problem wearing a hardware costume. A device outside your management platform is usually also outside your endpoint detection, your log collection, and whatever cybersecurity monitoring you already pay for. No alert fires because nothing is watching. Age matters mainly because it lowers the cost of entry; the damage lands in Microsoft 365, in finance systems, in whatever the stolen session can reach.

That reframes the decision. Hunting down every forgotten Windows 10 machine is worth doing and will never be complete, because you cannot prove you found the last one. Making device health a condition of access can be completed. If reaching company data requires a managed, known, compliant device, the machine you never found stops being a way in, because it no longer opens anything. Microsoft's guidance on token theft tells organizations to require a compliant device for all resources. Set a minimum operating system build in the compliance policy, and a lapsed update entitlement that used to be silent becomes a blocked sign-in somebody notices.

The design has limits worth stating. It covers services that authenticate through your identity platform, so a machine on the office network still needs network controls. Every exclusion in the policy is a door. A token stolen from a compliant device can still be replayed unless token protection or a network restriction applies. It also carries real friction and real project cost, and it deserves a deliberate decision rather than an aspirational one. In our judgment it is the control in this discussion least likely to decay.

Some machines genuinely cannot move

Part of the remaining Windows 10 population is not going anywhere. The vendor never certified the application for Windows 11. The instrument controller is locked to a validated configuration. A replacement exists but sits in next year's capital plan.

These are legitimate business positions. The failure is leaving them undocumented, which quietly converts a managed exception into ordinary drift. An exception worth accepting has four attributes: a named owner, a written compensating control, a replacement date, and a review scheduled on that date. The controls themselves are unglamorous and well understood.

  • Isolate the device on its own network segment with explicit rules about what it may reach, and deny general internet egress. If it carries extended updates, plan how it will activate and receive them from there.
  • Remove email and web browsing entirely. Many of these machines serve exactly one application.
  • Give it credentials that work nowhere else, and never a shared administrative account.
  • Image the full machine and verify the restore, so rebuilding it becomes a disaster recovery exercise rather than an archaeological one. Some of these configurations cannot be rebuilt from documentation, because the documentation does not exist.

Three answers to have in writing before the next renewal

The useful move is to settle three things on paper rather than to run another hardware count.

First: who owns the device inventory, and when was it last reconciled against devices observed on the network rather than devices listed in a spreadsheet? Second: which extended-update enrollments expire, on what date, and which named person receives that notice? Put Windows 11 on the same calendar, because its releases expire too; version 24H2 of Home and Pro left support on October 13, 2026. Third, and this is the one that outlasts the others: can a device you have never seen successfully sign in to your Microsoft 365 tenant today?

Worth doing alongside those: pull your own cyber insurance application and read what it asked about endpoint operating systems. Somebody in your organization answered that question, and the accuracy of the answer rests on an inventory that may not have been re-run since the upgrade project closed. Better to confirm it before a claim than during one.

The operating system was the deadline. Access is the decision, and it is still open.
