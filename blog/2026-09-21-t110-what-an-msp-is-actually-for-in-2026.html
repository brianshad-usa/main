Your MSP Contract Counts the Wrong Thing

IT agreements still price devices while blast radius moved to identity, SaaS and AI consent. Five questions to ask a provider before your next renewal.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 21, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The agreement counts endpoints. The incident starts in a consent screen.
  2. Availability is the floor, not the product
  3. Shadow AI is a procurement failure before it is a security failure
  4. Five renewal questions that reveal which decade a provider works in
  5. What you are actually buying now

Ask your IT leadership two questions at the next meeting. How many distinct AI tools did our staff use last month, and which of them can read company data? Then wait. If what comes back is an estimate rather than a list, the interesting part is that nothing in the way IT is scoped, contracted, or measured was designed to produce that list.

Stated as opinion, because it is one: that gap is the clearest way to understand what a managed services provider (MSP) is for in 2026. The traditional scope of devices, patches, uptime and tickets describes a company shaped the way companies were shaped when the scope was written. The work moved. The contract did not. This article is written for the executive who signs the renewal. A disclosure belongs with it: Pro Link Systems provides managed services, so weigh the argument with that interest in mind and put the questions below to any provider, this one included.

The agreement counts endpoints. The incident starts in a consent screen.

Open your current IT agreement and read what it enumerates. Most likely workstations, servers, perhaps network gear, priced per seat or per device. That structure was sound when the boundary of the business was a building with a firewall at the edge and company-owned laptops inside it.

Consider where an incident can begin now. A user approves a sign-in they did not initiate. Somewhere in the tenant, a third-party application holds a consent grant nobody reviewed. An engineer created a service principal for an integration two years ago, and its permissions have not been examined since. A stolen session token is replayed from another country. None of that touches the endpoint in the way endpoint tooling expects, and none of it appears on a device inventory.

The practical consequence, in our judgment: for a company whose work lives in a cloud tenant, a Conditional Access policy does more to set the blast radius than any firewall rule, and it appears nowhere on an invoice. Non-human identities (service accounts, API keys, app registrations, integration tokens) are the part of that perimeter with no manager and no offboarding date. Microsoft says of these workload identities that they cannot perform multifactor authentication, often have no formal lifecycle process, and need to store their credentials somewhere. If your organization has counted its own, you are ahead of the conversation, because a device-priced agreement would never produce that count. A provider whose cybersecurity scope is defined by agents installed on hardware is defending an earlier version of your company.

Availability is the floor, not the product

First-response time, first-contact resolution, average resolution, uptime: providers publish these figures and should be held to them. They are also the floor. Responsiveness from a help desk is what a provider owes you before the conversation about value begins. It is necessary, easy to measure, and not the reason to hire one in 2026.

What is scarce is judgment applied consistently. Somebody has to decide what Copilot in Microsoft 365 should be able to draw on, which requires understanding that it surfaces only what the individual user already has permission to view. It grants no new access. It makes existing access efficient. Suppose a SharePoint site was over-shared years ago because sharing was faster than structuring. A well-phrased question will now surface that content to anyone already entitled to it. Nothing is attacked. No control fails. The system does precisely what it was configured to do, and sensitive material walks into a summary.

That is not a product defect. It is a governance decision nobody owned, discovered long after it was made.

Shadow AI is a procurement failure before it is a security failure

The instinct when shadow AI comes up is to reach for blocking. Analysis, drawn from how earlier waves of consumer software entered the enterprise: blocking on its own does not hold, because the tool arrives on a personal card and a browser tab, and the person using it is trying to finish their work faster.

Watch the actual mechanism of loss. Nobody exfiltrates anything. An employee pastes a client contract into a general-purpose assistant to get a plain-language summary. Finance uploads a payroll export to build a forecast. A sales manager connects a note-taking tool that asks to read the calendar, with every invitation, attachment and attendee list on it. Each of those actions is reasonable in isolation. Together they form a stack of consent screens no governance process ever saw.

How much an employee can grant alone depends on a tenant setting worth reading aloud in the renewal meeting. Under Microsoft's managed default, which new tenants start with, users cannot consent on their own to third-party apps that want their mail, calendar or chats, or all the files and sites they can open, with an exception for popular mail clients. Older or customized settings may still allow it. Tightening the setting does not undo the past either: existing grants remain unchanged until someone revokes them.

The response that works is unglamorous: a sanctioned path that says yes quickly. Maintain a short list of approved tools with enterprise data terms. Publish an intake process with a real turnaround time. Route the requests users cannot approve to a named administrator through the admin consent workflow. Governance that only says no gets routed around, and then the exposure is unchanged with less visibility than before.

Five renewal questions that reveal which decade a provider works in

If you want an honest read on where your provider operates, the renewal meeting is the place to find out. Ask for the evidence along with the answer.

Five renewal questions and the evidence behind a good answer
QuestionEvidence worth seeingWhat a weak answer tells you
Who owns our identity control plane, and what can change in it without my approval?A written change process for Conditional Access, with the last three changes and who approved each.If nobody can describe the process, there is not one.
What is our inventory of non-human identities?Service accounts, API keys, app registrations and integration tokens, each with a named owner.A shrug may mark your largest unmanaged attack surface.
If ransomware landed tonight, what proves the backups survive it, and when was a restore last tested end to end?A disaster recovery test record with a date on it, and proof that a compromised administrator cannot delete the copies. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) notes that many ransomware variants try to find and delete or encrypt accessible backups.A backup that has never been restored is a belief, not a capability.
What is the approval path for a new AI tool, and how many business days does it take?An intake form, an owner, and the last few turnaround times.That number is the speed at which shadow AI stops being necessary.
Which of our SaaS tenants has no active administrator?A list of applications bought by departments, with a current administrator for each.Those still holding live data are worth finding first.

Notice what none of these questions ask about. Ticket volume does not appear. Neither does uptime percentage, nor the count of devices under management. Those figures describe effort, and effort was never the thing you were buying.

What you are actually buying now

Here is the honest description of a modern managed IT relationship: accountable ownership of a control plane you cannot see, exercised by people who make the same decision the same way every time, and who can explain that decision to an insurer, an auditor, or a board.

That is a different purchase than labor by the endpoint. It changes who you should be talking to at the provider, what belongs in the quarterly review, and what belongs in the agreement itself. Joint guidance from CISA and its international counterparts makes the contractual point: customers should confirm that the agreement specifies whether the provider or the customer owns specific responsibilities, and should understand the access the provider holds. If your reviews are dominated by ticket counts and hardware refresh schedules, the relationship is competently serving a version of your business that has already been replaced.

A test takes an hour. Print the scope schedule and mark every line as a device, an activity, or a decision. If no decisions appear, that is the amendment to negotiate, starting with who owns the identities that are not people. Our expectation, offered as opinion, is that the companies handling the next few years well will be those where a named person is accountable for the decisions their tools encode, and where the executive team knows who that person is.
