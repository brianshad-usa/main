Your IT Budget Runs on Four Clocks

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  October 21, 2026

October's IT number and February's IT number are rarely the same number. What is unusual is how seldom anyone can explain the difference in a single sentence. No invoice was approved twice. Nothing was overspent in a way a controller would flag. The line simply behaved differently than the spreadsheet assumed it would.

That gap is the defining feature of a 2026 technology budget, and it is not a discipline problem. Call it a modeling problem instead. A meaningful share of technology spend has stopped being a price and started being a behavior, and behaviors do not hold still for twelve months because a board packet asked them to.

For a Los Angeles business between 20 and 500 seats, the useful question is not how much should we spend on IT. It is narrower: what is each line actually indexed to? Most budgets at this size index everything to one variable — headcount. That assumption held for years. It is now wrong on at least three lines, and the errors run in different directions, which is why they do not cancel out.

Headcount Stopped Being the Denominator

Per-seat math is seductive because it is auditable. Users times dollars equals budget. Hire ten people, add ten seats, move on.

AI tooling broke the arithmetic — not because licenses are expensive, but because value lands unevenly across the company and usage lands unevenly across the license. Picture a forty-person professional services firm. A handful of people do work an assistant genuinely accelerates. Many others open it twice and forget it exists. Buying forty seats produces a clean budget line and a poor return. Six seats produce a messy line and a defensible one. The illustration is hypothetical. The pattern is worth testing against the usage reporting your tenant already generates.

Second-order effects arrive next. Heavy users start asking for things a seat does not cover: a workflow that runs without a human present, a connector into the line-of-business system, a document repository the assistant is permitted to read. Those requests do not scale with hiring. They scale with ambition, which has no cap and no hiring freeze.

Analysis rather than measurement: the companies that get this wrong in 2026 will not be the ones that spend too much. They will be the ones that spend evenly, across everyone, because even spending is easier to defend in a meeting than concentrated spending that actually works.

Your Identity Count Left the Org Chart Behind

Security spend is widely assumed to track headcount as well. It tracks identities, and the identity population in a mid-size company stopped resembling the org chart some time ago.

Count what holds credentials in your environment. Employees and contractors, certainly. Then the outsourced bookkeeper, the agency with access to your marketing platform, every service account created so one system can talk to another, every API key issued to an integration nobody has reviewed since installation, every automation running under a machine identity with standing permissions. Run that count before assuming the employee list is still the largest part of it.

Budgeting cares about this because identity is where most practical cybersecurity work now happens. Conditional Access policy, privileged access review, passkey rollout, offboarding that revokes tokens rather than merely disabling a mailbox — that labor scales with the number and complexity of identities, not with staff count. A company that hires nobody and adopts four integrated platforms has raised its security workload without moving a single denominator anyone is watching.

The practical move is unglamorous. Ahead of the next cycle, ask for an inventory of non-human identities and the permissions attached to them. The list usually runs longer than the executive team expects, and it is the most honest input available to a security budget.

Metered Lines Need a Governor, Not a Forecast

Three categories of spend are now metered rather than licensed: cloud infrastructure, AI usage, and the long tail of departmental software that never passed through IT at all.

Forecasting metered spend precisely wastes a finance team's time, because a forecast is a guess about behavior twelve months out. What a metered line needs instead is a governor: visibility, a ceiling, a named owner, and an alert when the slope changes. That is a different control structure from a forecast, and most small and mid-size companies have not built one, because the amounts were historically small enough to absorb.

Absorption stops working around the point where Microsoft 365 add-ons, storage growth, an AI assistant, and a handful of departmental tools all compound at once. Three questions are worth asking about every metered line before the year starts:

  • Who sees this number monthly, by name, without having to request a report?
  • What is the ceiling, and what happens automatically as it is approached?
  • If this line doubles, is that failure or success — and could we tell the difference from the invoice alone?

That last question separates cost control from cost blindness. A doubled AI line in a team that shipped twice the work is a good outcome. The same doubling beside flat output is a signal nobody reads, because it arrives as a number rather than as a comparison.

The Obligations You Never Negotiated

One category barely existed in a 2016 budget. Call it obligation spend: controls implemented because an insurer, a client, a lender, or a regulator requires them, not because the priority was chosen internally.

Los Angeles businesses feel this pressure unevenly. A post-production company answers security addenda from studio clients. A medical practice carries HIPAA obligations that reach the vendors handling patient data on its behalf. A law firm answers client questionnaires about retention and access. A Valley manufacturer answers its largest customer's supply-chain assessment. None of those arrive on a schedule matching a fiscal year, and each converts directly into technology work.

Opinion, stated as such: obligation spend belongs in the plan as a standing allowance rather than discovered as an emergency. Multi-factor authentication, logging and retention, immutable data backup with tested recovery, documented access review — these recur across questionnaire after questionnaire. Funding them ahead of the question costs less than funding them during a contract negotiation in which the counterparty controls the clock.

Four Clocks, One Number

A budget that survives the year is not one with better forecasts. It is one where every line carries two pieces of information the spreadsheet usually omits: what the line is indexed to, and who owns the variance when the index moves.

Headcount lines move when you hire. Identity lines move when you integrate. Consumption lines move when people use the tools more, which may be the best news in the document. Obligation lines move when somebody outside the company decides they move. Four clocks, four owners, and one total that finally explains itself in February.

A fifth line gets mispriced in both directions: support labor. Under-fund it and the cost returns as unmeasured executive time. Over-fund it against a vendor that cannot answer the phone and you have bought a receipt. The real question is not the rate but the behavior the rate buys — whether a call reaches a person, and how quickly work actually starts.
