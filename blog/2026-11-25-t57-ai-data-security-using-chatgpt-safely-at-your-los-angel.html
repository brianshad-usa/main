Who Owns Your Company's AI History?

Most AI policies police what staff paste. The bigger 2026 exposure is whose account holds the work and what it can reach.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
November 25, 2026
6 min read
In this article
  1. The leak most AI policies miss is the account, not the prompt
  2. Connected assistants work with the permissions your staff already have
  3. Why banning ChatGPT tends to increase exposure
  4. What a company-owned AI workspace changes
  5. One question leadership has to answer on purpose

Key takeaways

  • The larger AI exposure now is whose account holds the work and what that account can reach, not only what employees paste into a prompt.
  • Connected assistants generally act with each user's existing permissions, so cleaning up overshared files and locking down app consent come before any AI rollout.
  • A blanket ban tends to push AI use out of sight. A company-owned, identity-managed workspace with approved connectors is the defensible path.

A sales director resigns on a Friday. IT disables her email, collects her laptop and removes her from the shared drives. Everything looks closed. Nobody touches the personal ChatGPT account she has used for two years to draft proposals, summarize client calls and rework pricing. That history leaves with her: your customers, your margins and your negotiating positions, held in an account your company never owned and cannot audit.

The scenario is hypothetical, but nothing in it is unusual. This is the default state of any business that has not made a deliberate decision about AI accounts. The scenario also points to a shift most executives have not yet absorbed. What employees type into a chatbot still matters. The bigger question now is whose account the work lives in, and what that account is allowed to reach.

The leak most AI policies miss is the account, not the prompt

The first generation of AI guidance was written in a hurry and focused on the paste: do not put confidential data into ChatGPT. That advice was reasonable, and it is now incomplete. It treats each prompt as a one-time disclosure. In practice, an assistant becomes a working archive. Conversations accumulate, files get uploaded, and memory features can carry context across sessions. Over months, a single account can hold a more coherent picture of a business than any one folder on the file server.

When that account is personal, three consequences follow. As a matter of fact, the data is governed by the consumer terms the employee accepted, not by any agreement your company signed. By analysis, two more follow. You have no visibility into what was shared, and no way to retrieve or delete it when the person leaves. Offboarding, the control most companies trust to end access, does not apply, because the company never had access to end. That last point tends to surprise leadership most.

For this reason, AI data security belongs inside identity management rather than beside it. The discipline that governs who can sign into email should also govern who can sign into the AI tools that touch company data.

Connected assistants work with the permissions your staff already have

The second change is less visible and more consequential. Assistants no longer wait for someone to paste text into a box. They connect to mailboxes, calendars, cloud drives and document libraries, and newer agent features can take actions across those systems on a user's behalf. When an employee authorizes a connector, the assistant generally operates with that employee's access.

That sounds contained until you consider what permissions actually look like inside most organizations. Some folders were shared with everyone years ago. Former project members were never removed. A finance spreadsheet was linked openly so a vendor could see it once. People rarely stumble across these gaps, because finding them takes effort. An assistant that can search everything a user can see removes that effort. The oversharing was already there; the assistant makes it searchable.

The same logic applies to Copilot inside Microsoft 365, which works within each user's existing access. That is why permission cleanup has become the first step of any responsible rollout. A newer category of risk also deserves a name. An assistant that reads incoming email or browses the web can be fed instructions hidden inside that content, a technique security researchers call prompt injection. For an executive, the implication is simple: the more an assistant can do without asking, the more tightly its reach should be limited.

Why banning ChatGPT tends to increase exposure

Faced with all this, some leadership teams reach for a prohibition. In our view, a blanket ban is usually the least safe option available. These tools save people enough time that a ban rarely ends the habit. More often it relocates it to phones, personal laptops and home networks, where the company sees nothing. A risk you could have managed becomes shadow AI you cannot see.

Substitution works better. Give people a sanctioned tool that is at least as capable as the one they would choose on their own. Attach it to company identity, and make the approved path the easiest one. A written policy then has something to point to besides a prohibition.

What a company-owned AI workspace changes

For most organizations of 20 to 500 seats, the core decision is moving AI work out of personal accounts and into a workspace the company owns. OpenAI and other vendors offer business tiers with different data-handling terms and administrative controls than consumer accounts. Those terms and feature sets change, and some controls depend on the plan, so read the current agreement rather than relying on last year's. The structural differences are more stable:

Personal versus company-managed AI accounts
QuestionPersonal accountCompany-managed workspace
Who controls the conversation historyThe employeeThe company, under its workspace settings
What happens when someone leavesThe history leaves with themAccess can end with the company identity when sign-on is centralized
Which terms govern the dataConsumer terms the individual acceptedThe business agreement your company signed
Who approves connectorsEach individual, case by caseAn administrator, by policy
Visibility into usageNoneAdministrative controls, depending on plan

Owning the workspace is the foundation. These decisions complete it:

  • Require single sign-on through your existing identity provider where the plan supports it. AI access is then granted and revoked along with everything else, and Conditional Access policies apply to it.
  • Restrict employees from granting third-party apps access to company mail and files on their own, and route those requests to an administrator. This closes the side door a personal account would otherwise use.
  • Decide which connectors are permitted, and who approves new ones, before staff authorize them one at a time.
  • Clean up overshared files and mailboxes before switching on assistant search across them.
  • Name the short list of data that never enters any AI tool, whatever the tier.
  • Require a human to confirm before an agent sends, deletes or pays anything.

Industry shapes that short list, and in Los Angeles that often means entertainment and legal work: unreleased scripts, cuts and deal terms at studios and production companies, privileged material at law firms. Medical practices add patient information. There, which tools may touch the data is a question for your compliance advisor first, and the healthcare IT configuration follows from that answer.

One question leadership has to answer on purpose

Governing this well does not require becoming an AI expert. Executives do need to answer one question deliberately rather than by default: where does our company's AI work live, and what can it reach? Suppose the honest answer is a few dozen personal accounts, each connected to whatever its owner chose to authorize. In that case, the exposure already exists, whatever the policy document says.

The remedy is unglamorous. Own the accounts, tie them to identity, approve the connectors and clean up the permissions underneath. None of it requires waiting for new regulation or for the tools to mature. This is ordinary cybersecurity discipline applied to a new kind of system. Organizations whose internal teams or managed IT provider already practice identity hygiene will find that most of the work extends controls they already run.

One prediction, offered as a prediction: which accounts hold a company's AI history will become a routine question in vendor due diligence and insurance conversations. Leadership teams that have already answered it will spend that conversation on strategy instead of remediation.
