AI Governance Is a Permissions Problem

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  October 25, 2026

Ask a Los Angeles executive whether the company has adopted AI and you will usually get a measured answer. A pilot in marketing. A committee forming. A Copilot trial finance is evaluating. Ask a narrower question — which of the software products you already pay for shipped an AI feature in the past year — and the honest answer is close to all of them.

That gap is the governance problem in one sentence. Adoption was decided for you, incrementally, by vendors pushing features into products you had already licensed. What remains yours to decide is narrower and far more consequential: what those features are permitted to reach.

The policy document is not the control

Most AI governance work still begins the way it began in 2023, with a document. Acceptable use. Prohibited data classes. A paragraph about verifying outputs. Such documents are not useless, but executives consistently overestimate what they accomplish, because a policy governs intent and very little in an AI system fails on intent.

Consider the actual failure modes. An employee pastes a client list into a consumer chatbot because the approved tool was slower. A meeting assistant joins a call nobody deliberately invited it to and transcribes a conversation about a pending transaction. Someone asks an honest question and receives a document they were never meant to see. The first is a usability failure, the second a configuration default, the third a permissions error that predates AI by a decade.

This is analysis rather than fact, but it survives examination: a policy binds only the small fraction of AI risk that runs through a deliberate human choice. Everything else runs through identity, configuration, and defaults — the same places the rest of your cybersecurity controls already live.

An assistant finds what was already exposed

The most common misunderstanding about Microsoft Copilot and comparable enterprise assistants is that they create a new data exposure. Generally they do not. They inherit the permissions of the person asking and return what that person could already have found.

The weight sits on the phrase could already have found. In a business that has run Microsoft 365 for eight or ten years, that phrase covers an enormous and largely unexamined surface. A departed executive's OneDrive folder shared to All Company. A SharePoint site stood up for a 2019 acquisition and never locked back down. Payroll spreadsheets opened to a wider group for one quarter and never closed. Link sharing set to anyone in the organization because that was the fastest path at the time.

None of it was ever safe. It was merely impractical to discover. Finding a stray compensation file used to require knowing it existed and knowing where to look; an assistant removes both requirements. It searches every location the asker can technically reach, synthesizes what it finds, and answers in plain language — instantly, politely, with no sense that the answer should not exist.

So the sequence of the project inverts. Most plans treat permissions remediation as preliminary cleanup to get through before the interesting AI work begins. Treat it instead as the governance project itself. The assistant is simply the thing that makes a decade of accumulated sharing debt legible.

Agents move the unit of governance from tool to identity

Through 2024 and 2025, most business AI was conversational. A person asked, a model answered, the person decided what to do next. The human stayed in the loop because the human was the only thing capable of acting.

That boundary is dissolving, and what follows is prediction rather than fact: within the next two budget cycles, most mid-sized companies will be running software agents that take actions on their behalf — read a mailbox, update a record, file a ticket, advance a step in a workflow. An agent that acts needs credentials. Credentials mean an identity. Identity means everything your organization already knows about access control now applies to something that is not a person and does not behave like one.

Blunt framing for a board: an agent is a worker with broad capability, no judgment, no sense of consequence, and no personnel file. It will not notice that a request is unusual. Nothing about it triggers the social instincts that make human insider risk partially self-limiting.

The governance questions follow directly, and they are old questions in new clothes:

  • Who owns this identity? A named human accountable for its behavior, not a department.
  • What is its scope? The narrowest set of systems and data that lets it do one job — not the admin rights that made setup faster.
  • Who approved the connection? Every integration an agent holds into a third-party SaaS product is a supply-chain dependency that inherits that vendor's security posture.
  • Is it logged where you can see it? Agent actions visible only inside a vendor console are invisible to your own monitoring.
  • Can you revoke it this afternoon? If the answer begins with finding whoever set it up, that is not a control. It is a hope.

Note what is missing from that list: which model is used, how it was trained, whether it hallucinates. Those questions matter for output quality. They are not where the operational risk concentrates.

What a short AI policy actually decides

Long AI policies fail for the same reason long password policies failed. Nobody reads them, and they push people toward whatever is faster.

A policy that survives contact with a real workweek makes decisions instead of listing prohibitions. Name the tier of sanctioned tools where company data is permitted, and make those tools good enough that nobody needs an alternative. State the two or three data categories that never leave sanctioned systems — client records, financials, anything carrying HIPAA compliance obligations — in language a new hire can apply without calling counsel. Designate one accountable owner for AI decisions, probably not the most enthusiastic person in the building. Require that any agent with write access to a production system be registered somewhere a human can audit it. Give employees a path to ask about a new tool and get an answer in days, because a governance process slower than the business simply gets routed around.

Shadow AI is rarely defiance. It is almost always an efficiency gap. An IT support function that answers quickly and says yes often is a stronger control than a policy that says no slowly — one reason Pro Link Systems keeps its help desk in-house, US-based, and staffed 24/7 rather than outsourced.

Start with two artifacts, not a document

If AI governance is on the agenda this quarter, resist the instinct to begin with drafting. Produce two artifacts first: a permissions audit of your Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace tenant, and an inventory of every AI feature already live in software you pay for. Both are tedious. Both will tell you more about your real exposure than any policy you could write, and they will reorder your priorities in ways the drafting exercise never does.

Governing AI well looks remarkably like governing identity well — the work most organizations deferred while it was merely important.
