Backups That Survive Your Own Admins

Immutable backups fail the moment an administrator can shorten retention. The 2026 recovery question is who holds the delete path, not where copies live.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 7, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. Encryption is loud. Deletion is patient.
  2. One question decides whether a backup is actually immutable
  3. Microsoft 365 retention is one blast radius, not two
  4. Clean data restored into a compromised directory is not recovery
  5. The number to put in front of your board

The most valuable credential in a 200-seat company may not be the domain administrator's. It may belong to whoever administers the backup platform. That account can often make every copy of the business unrecoverable in a single afternoon, with no malware deployed, no files encrypted and no endpoint alert raised.

Something shifted in the ransomware economy while many recovery plans stayed where they were. Encryption used to be the attack. Now encryption is the invoice. The work that actually decides the outcome happens earlier and quieter: get in, find the backup console, and remove the thing that would have let the company say no. CISA's ransomware guide warns that many variants look for accessible backups in order to delete or encrypt them. This essay is for the executives who approve the recovery budget and the IT leaders who defend it. The decision it informs is who is allowed to shorten the life of a backup.

Encryption is loud. Deletion is patient.

Detection tooling has become good at spotting bulk encryption. Mass file rewrites, shadow copy deletion, unusual process behavior: that is precisely the signal endpoint detection platforms are tuned to catch. An intruder who intends to be paid has every reason to delay that stage as long as possible.

Consider an alternative path that requires no exploit at all. This is an illustrative scenario, not an incident report. A credential with rights to the backup platform is phished. Nothing is visibly deleted. Retention is simply reduced from ninety days to three. Jobs keep running. Dashboards stay green. The nightly success email arrives on time. Recovery capability then expires on schedule, by design, through the platform's own normal operation. Nobody deleted a backup. The system did exactly what an authorized administrator instructed it to do.

There is a more mundane version of the same weakness. Backup products need broad read access. Older designs sign in as a user account that has been excluded from Conditional Access because multifactor prompts break the job, and the exclusion sits in a policy's exclusion list, where reviews tend to skim past it. Newer designs connect as an application, and policies scoped to users do not apply to applications at all. Microsoft's Conditional Access for workload identities is licensed separately and does not reach third-party or multitenant apps. Our opinion, stated as opinion: machine identities carrying standing privilege with no adaptive controls are among the least examined risks in mid-market environments, and the backup identity is often one of the most powerful in the building.

One question decides whether a backup is actually immutable

"Immutable" has become a datasheet checkbox, which means it now describes several materially different things. The distinction that matters is not where copies live, how many exist, or whether the storage is described as write-once. It is this: who can shorten retention before the clock runs out?

If the answer includes any human being (an internal administrator with sufficient privilege, a vendor support engineer acting on a ticket, a cloud account owner with a policy override) then the data is durable rather than immutable. Durable survives hardware failure and accident. Immutable survives an adversary holding legitimate credentials. Those are different products solving different problems, and the second is what a credential-driven threat model requires. Vendors' own documentation draws the line, if you read past the datasheet:

Four questions produce an honest answer in about ten minutes, and whoever manages your data backup should be able to answer them without hedging:

  • Can any single administrator in our organization reduce retention or delete a locked recovery point? If so, name them.
  • Can the vendor's support team override the lock at our request, and therefore at an attacker's request, if that attacker sounds like us on the phone?
  • Is the backup platform's authentication separate from the production directory, with its own privileged access controls?
  • When was a restore last performed and documented? A job status check does not count.

The fourth question tends to be the uncomfortable one.

Microsoft 365 retention is one blast radius, not two

A common and expensive misunderstanding holds that native retention features constitute a backup. Recycle bins, retention policies, litigation hold, and versioning are all genuinely useful. They are also administered from inside the same tenant, by the same privileged roles, under the same identity plane an attacker may have just compromised. Unless a policy carries a Preservation Lock, a sufficiently privileged administrator can modify it. That is not a flaw in the platform. It is what administration means.

Which is why Microsoft 365 deserves an independent, separately authenticated copy of mail, files and Teams content, and increasingly of the configuration state of the tenant itself. Part of that last item now comes built in. Entra takes a daily backup of supported directory objects, keeps up to seven days with a P1 or P2 license, and does not let tenant administrators alter or delete it. Seven days is a short window when an intruder has been patient, so it complements an outside copy rather than replacing one. Analysis rather than fact: as processes move into SaaS and AI agents act on that data under delegated permissions, many mid-market companies have accumulated single-blast-radius data faster than they have redrawn their recovery architecture to match.

Clean data restored into a compromised directory is not recovery

Assume the immutable copies held and everything is recoverable. Here is where recovery plans written before identity became the perimeter start to fail: restore into what?

If the intrusion touched the directory, and an attack that runs on stolen credentials should be assumed to have, then restoring servers and mailboxes into that same directory reintroduces the attacker's persistence alongside the data. NIST's recovery guide tells organizations to check assumptions such as the trustworthiness of directory services as recovery starts, and notes that an identity or authentication server often has to come back first. A clean identity plane has to exist before restored workloads can safely attach to it, which makes the rebuild path for identity a recovery asset in its own right: documented, tested, and stored somewhere the incident cannot reach.

Two failure modes are easy to predict and easy to prevent. The runbook lives in the SharePoint site that is currently unavailable. The break-glass credentials live in a password manager that authenticates through the compromised directory. Both are simple to fix in advance and nearly impossible to fix mid-incident. Treating the recovery procedure as a protected artifact rather than as documentation is a cybersecurity decision, not a filing decision.

The number to put in front of your board

Storage cost is the wrong axis for this decision. Locked copies cost more mainly because nobody can prune them early, and that difference is not the figure that matters. The figure that matters is time-to-first-clean-login: how long from the declaration of an incident until employees are working in a directory nobody else controls, with their data intact. That number is measurable only by rehearsal, and in our judgment it is usually longer than executives assume.

So the budget question is whether the deletion path has been closed against a credentialed adversary, whether the identity rebuild is written down somewhere reachable, and whether anyone has proven a restore recently with evidence a cyber insurer or an auditor would accept. Whether that work sits with internal staff or an outside managed IT partner matters far less than whether a named person owns it. Three honest answers describe an organization's resilience better than any dashboard, because the dashboard's job is to report that the system did what it was told.
