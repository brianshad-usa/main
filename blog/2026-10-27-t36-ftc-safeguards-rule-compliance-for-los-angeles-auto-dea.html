Your Breach Notice Is Now Public Record

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  October 27, 2026

A regulator with a clipboard working the F&I office is the version of Safeguards Rule risk most dealer principals and consumer finance executives carry in their heads. It is also the least likely version to occur. The realistic path to an enforcement conversation does not begin with an examination. It begins with a filing you submit about yourself.

That is the structural change many covered businesses have not absorbed. The Safeguards Rule, part of Gramm-Leach-Bliley and codified at 16 CFR Part 314, has long reached well past banks — auto dealers that arrange financing or leasing, mortgage brokers, consumer finance companies, collection agencies, tax preparers. Coverage is not the new part. The aftermath is.

Thirty days, 500 consumers, one public database

Effective May 13, 2024, the FTC's amended Safeguards Rule requires a covered financial institution to notify the Commission of a notification event as soon as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery, when the event involves the unencrypted customer information of at least 500 consumers. The report identifies the institution, describes the types of information involved, gives the date or date range of the event, and states the number of consumers affected or potentially affected. Under the rule, those reports are entered into a publicly available database.

Read that sequence as an executive rather than as a compliance officer. The Commission is not the only audience. A floorplan lender is an audience. So is a captive finance partner, a cyber insurer pricing a renewal, a buy-side team running diligence on your group, and any reporter who thinks to check the list. This is rule text rather than forecast: the disclosure obligation now terminates in a public record.

A second provision deserves more attention than it gets. Unauthorized acquisition is presumed when an unauthorized person has accessed unencrypted customer information, unless the institution holds reliable evidence that acquisition did not occur and could not reasonably have occurred. The burden sits on your side of the table. In practice, the quality of your logging and log retention determines whether you can rebut the presumption, or whether you notify by default because you cannot establish what an intruder touched.

Encryption became the line between contained and published

The trigger is unencrypted customer information. Encryption at rest and in transit is already required by the rule, with a narrow allowance for effective alternative compensating controls reviewed and approved in writing by the Qualified Individual. The 2024 amendment gave encryption a second job: separating an incident you manage privately from an incident the market can look up.

Analysis follows. Most dealer groups and finance firms do not have an encryption problem in the core system of record. They have one in everything orbiting it — credit application PDFs resting in a shared mailbox, deal jackets scanned to a file share, a spreadsheet a manager built with customer names attached, a backup appliance in a back office nobody has examined since the vendor racked it. Those repositories are what produce a 500-consumer notification event, and they are the least likely to appear in any inventory.

The rule also treats information as unencrypted if the encryption key was accessed by an unauthorized person. That detail collapses a comfortable assumption. If one compromised credential can reach production data, the key material, and the backup copy, encryption stops functioning as a defense at the exact moment it is needed. A disaster recovery design with that property creates a disclosure problem, not only a recovery problem.

The Qualified Individual you did not actually outsource

The rule requires a designated Qualified Individual responsible for overseeing, implementing, and enforcing the information security program, and it permits that person to be employed by an affiliate or a service provider. What it does not permit is the handoff many executives believe they completed. The institution retains full responsibility, and it must designate a senior member of its own personnel to direct and oversee the third party.

Often that senior member is a CFO or COO whose name sits on a program they have never read — an analytical observation, not a measured one, but a pattern worth testing inside your own organization. The rule further requires the Qualified Individual to report in writing at least annually to a board, equivalent governing body, or senior officer, covering program status and compliance, risk assessment results, service provider arrangements, testing results, security events and management's response, and recommendations for change. That report is the first artifact an enforcement inquiry will ask to see. It is frequently the one that does not exist.

Worth knowing: institutions maintaining customer information concerning fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from several provisions, including the written risk assessment, the incident response plan, and the annual report. Few operating dealerships or finance companies with any history fall under that line. Assuming you do is a decision, and it deserves to be a documented one.

The AI tools that became service providers without paperwork

Now the 2026 layer. Over the past two years, sales and service organizations have adopted AI quickly and informally — chat agents qualifying leads, voice agents absorbing BDC overflow, CRM features summarizing customer conversations, assistants embedded in Microsoft 365 that index whatever a user can already open. Each one either processes nonpublic personal information or has line of sight to it.

The Safeguards Rule requires an institution to select and retain service providers capable of maintaining appropriate safeguards, to require those safeguards by contract, and to periodically assess them based on the risk they present. It requires an inventory of data, personnel, devices, systems, and facilities. A written risk assessment, periodically reassessed, is required as well. An AI vendor touching credit applications sits inside all three obligations, and the informal adoption pattern — a department head puts a tool on a card, it is live in a week — satisfies none of them.

Shadow AI sharpens the point. A salesperson pasting a customer's income and employment details into a consumer chatbot to draft a follow-up email has moved customer information to a processor your written program has never heard of. That is an ordinary governance gap wherever AI use lacks a policy, a sanctioned toolset, and a technical boundary. Opinion, clearly labeled: unreviewed AI tooling will become a routine item in Safeguards reviews well before most firms treat it as one.

What to change before the next renewal

The rule did not become harder to satisfy in 2024. The consequence of not satisfying it moved from a private regulatory matter into a public record, which changes who your compliance program is actually for. Three decisions follow.

  • Find the unencrypted copies. Not the system of record — the mailboxes, file shares, scan folders, and legacy backups where customer information accumulates outside anyone's design.
  • Name the senior person who oversees the Qualified Individual, and have that person read the annual written report before an incident rather than after one.
  • Route every AI tool through service provider review, and give staff a sanctioned option so the unsanctioned one stops being the convenient path.

None of this is exotic. Inventory, access control, encryption, logging, and a response plan someone has rehearsed are the same controls that make day-to-day managed IT operations stable. What is new is that the market now gets to see whether you had them.
