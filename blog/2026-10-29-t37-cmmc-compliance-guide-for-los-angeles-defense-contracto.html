CMMC Now Decides What You Can Bid

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  October 29, 2026

Ask a defense subcontractor in the South Bay what CMMC costs, and you will usually get a tooling estimate — a number for endpoint software, a number for a consultant, a number for the assessment itself. Ask what it costs to fail one, and the honest answer is the contract, plus the follow-on work that contract would have produced, plus a position in a prime's supplier list that took years to earn.

That reframing is the whole 2026 story. The Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification has stopped being a future obligation on a compliance roadmap and become a condition of award. The program works by inserting requirements into solicitations, which means the operative question is no longer when compliance becomes mandatory. It is which work your company can still bid on next quarter — a revenue question wearing an IT costume.

The Requirement Arrives in a Solicitation, Not in the Mail

There is no envelope. No inspector visits a Valencia fabricator or an El Segundo engineering firm and issues a finding. The mechanism is quieter: a solicitation carries a certification level, a prime flows the requirement down to its suppliers, and a company that cannot show the required status is simply not eligible for award. Market access disappears before anyone has a conversation about it.

The levels are worth stating plainly, because executives often inherit a muddled version from a vendor pitch. Level 1 concerns Federal Contract Information and relies on self-assessment against basic safeguarding requirements already written into federal contract clauses. Controlled Unclassified Information moves you to Level 2, which maps to NIST SP 800-171 and, for much of the defense base, is verified by a third-party assessor rather than by your own attestation. The highest level draws on NIST SP 800-172 and is assessed by the government itself. Which one applies to you is written in your contracts and your primes' flow-down language — not in an article, and not in a sales deck.

The practical consequence for a supplier of a few dozen people: the distance between Level 1 and Level 2 is not a difference of degree. One is an internal exercise. The other is an external examination of a defined environment by people whose job is to find the gaps. Firms that discover that distinction in month two of a six-month bid cycle do not make the date.

Scope Is the Only Cost Lever You Actually Control

Most guidance buries this. You cannot negotiate the security requirements. You can, within honest limits, decide how much of your company they apply to.

Scoping separates assets that handle CUI from security protection assets, from assets managed under contractor risk, from specialized equipment, from everything outside the boundary entirely. The architectural decision that follows drives cost, timeline, and permanent operational drag more than any product you will buy: harden the whole company, or build a bounded enclave where CUI lives and keep the rest of the business outside the assessment boundary.

Neither answer is universally correct, and this is analysis rather than doctrine. An engineering firm where nearly every project touches CUI may find an enclave adds more friction than it removes. A precision manufacturer where only a couple of programs involve CUI will likely regret applying defense-grade controls to the entire shop floor, the quoting process, and the front office. An enclave concentrates cost and audit surface. The enterprise approach concentrates convenience. Either way, the decision belongs on the CEO's desk, because it shapes how the business operates for the next decade.

Scope also settles your cloud posture. Where CUI is processed, stored, or transmitted by an outside service, the DFARS safeguarding clause already sitting in many defense contracts sets expectations for that provider against a federal security baseline — read the clause language in your own agreement rather than a summary of it. For a Los Angeles contractor, that converts an abstract compliance question into a concrete one: which Microsoft 365 tenant you actually run, what your email carries day to day, and whether the collaboration tools your project teams adopted on their own have ever been evaluated against that bar.

Someone's Name Goes on the Affirmation

CMMC requires an annual affirmation of continuing compliance, entered by a senior company official. A named human asserts, to the government, in connection with contracts that pay money, that the required controls are in place.

That is a different category of statement than an audit finding. Representations made to the government in connection with payment sit in the territory the False Claims Act governs, and your counsel — not your IT provider — should walk the signer through what that means. The structural point for an executive is simple enough: an inaccurate compliance claim is not merely something to remediate at the next assessment.

Two consequences follow. The affirming official needs a reporting line that produces evidence rather than reassurance, which means documentation and logs rather than a verbal yes from whoever answers the phone for IT support. Compliance also has to be continuous rather than seasonal. An assessment captures a moment; the affirmation covers a year. Configuration drift, a new vendor, an unmanaged laptop, a lapsed logging pipeline, a data backup job that quietly stopped verifying restores — each widens the gap between what was certified and what is true.

Generative AI Opened a Boundary Nobody Redrew

Older compliance guidance does not address the next part, and it deserves board attention.

Assessment boundaries were drawn around networks, endpoints, and named cloud services. Then employees started pasting work into AI assistants. An engineer summarizing a statement of work, a program manager drafting a reply to a government customer, a quality lead asking a chatbot to tidy up a procedure — any of these can move CUI into an external service that appears nowhere in the System Security Plan and has never been evaluated against a federal baseline.

Shadow AI inside a CMMC environment is not an abstract governance worry. It is an unapproved external service provider operating inside the boundary, invisible unless someone is specifically looking for it. The same holds for AI features switched on by default in tools you already own. A copilot that indexes a document library is doing exactly what it was built to do; if that library holds CUI, where the data then travels becomes an assessment question.

Prediction, labeled as one: AI tool governance will become a common source of friction in Level 2 assessments, because adoption has outrun policy almost everywhere. The defensible position is unglamorous — an explicit list of approved AI tools inside the CUI boundary, technical controls that block the rest, and a cybersecurity program that treats AI usage as monitored activity rather than personal preference.

What to Decide Before the Next Solicitation

Three decisions are worth making now.

  • Establish your actual level from your contracts, not from assumption. Read the flow-down language and ask your primes directly what they will require and when. Guessing high wastes capital; guessing low removes you from bids you were counting on.
  • Choose enclave or enterprise at the executive level. This is a business architecture choice with a ten-year tail, and delegating it downward reliably produces the most convenient answer rather than the one that is cheapest to live with.
  • Find your CUI before an assessor does. Most organizations are wrong about where it lives. It is in email threads, in file shares nobody has audited in years, on a laptop in a trunk on the 405, and increasingly in an AI chat history no one knew existed.

The uncomfortable part underneath all of it: certification rewards companies that can produce evidence, not companies that feel secure. Documentation, logs, and consistent operational discipline are what an assessment actually examines. The firms that struggle are rarely the ones with weak technology. They are the ones who cannot prove what their technology does — and proof accumulates over quarters, not in the month before an assessor arrives.
