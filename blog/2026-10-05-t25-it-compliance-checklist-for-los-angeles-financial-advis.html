Compliance Is Now an Evidence Problem

Advisory firms are asked to demonstrate controls, not describe them. What the rules ask you to show, and four checks that reveal whether you can.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
October 5, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article

  2. What the rules ask you to be able to show
  3. Identity is the control surface you can evidence
  4. Advice is migrating into tools your archive has never seen

An examiner asks one question: who holds administrative access to the system where client data moves between the CRM and the portfolio accounting platform, and when was that access last reviewed? In this constructed example the answer takes four days, three vendors, and a partner's weekend to assemble. Nothing was breached. No one was negligent. The firm genuinely performs the review. It simply could not show that the review was operating on the day in question.

That gap, between doing the thing and demonstrating the thing, is where IT compliance for a Los Angeles financial advisory firm lives in 2026. It is also where the standard checklist fails its reader. A list of controls tells you what to have. It does not tell you what you will be asked to show. This article is for the principal, chief compliance officer or operations lead who will be asked. It describes the rules in general terms; counsel should confirm how they apply to your registration.

The binder stopped being the artifact. The log became the artifact.

Written information security programs, incident response plans, vendor due diligence files: all still necessary, and no longer sufficient. In a September 2026 risk alert, the Division of Examinations at the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) described advisers that created documentation during their annual compliance reviews and did not maintain it, or whose own policies called for a written report that was never prepared. Its fiscal year 2027 priorities say examinations will review access controls, account management, and response and recovery protocols. These are staff statements rather than new rules. They are also a fair preview of the document request.

What has shifted, and this is analysis rather than a finding, is that three different parties now ask the same question in three dialects. The regulator asks whether the program is reasonably designed and actually followed. The insurer asks which controls are enforced everywhere, without exception. The institutional client's operational due diligence team asks for the artifacts by name.

Each of those is an evidence request. A policy stating that access reviews occur quarterly is a sentence. An exportable record showing which accounts were reviewed, by whom, and what was revoked is an artifact. Firms that treat IT security as a documentation exercise produce sentences. Operating discipline produces artifacts, and produces them in an afternoon.

Recoverability follows the same logic. A tested data backup is interesting less as a best practice than because a restore log turns "we can recover client records" into something a reader can check. Backup that has never been restored is a theory.

What the rules ask you to be able to show

The largest recent change for smaller firms is Regulation S-P. Its amendments apply to every SEC-registered adviser, and the compliance date for smaller entities was June 3, 2026. The table sets out the main sources in plain language. It is a map for a conversation with counsel, not a substitute for one.

Identity is the control surface you can evidence

For a twenty-to-eighty person wealth management firm, there is little meaningful network perimeter left. Custody sits at the custodian. Portfolio accounting is typically a SaaS platform. Email, documents and chat live in Microsoft 365. Planning software, CRM, e-signature and the client portal are four more vendors. The attack surface is not an office in Century City or Pasadena. It is a set of identities.

Business email compromise persists for structural reasons rather than exotic ones. An attacker holding a legitimate session token needs no malware. They need patience, a mailbox rule that routes anything containing the word "wire" into a folder nobody opens, and a plausible moment to intervene in a payment that was already going to happen.

Controls that address this are unglamorous and testable. Conditional Access that gates sign-in on device compliance and location rather than merely prompting for a code. Phishing-resistant authentication, meaning passkeys or hardware keys, for anyone holding administrative rights or payment authority. Administrative accounts kept separate from daily-use accounts. Alerting on mailbox forwarding rules, a detection that maps to a fraud pattern rather than to a generic threat category.

Each of those leaves a record. A firm that has consolidated identity can answer from one place. Eleven disconnected logins produce eleven partial answers and no timeline. Check how long each log is kept, too, because a record that expired last month is not evidence.

Advice is migrating into tools your archive has never seen

The exposure many firms have not yet priced is simpler than it sounds. An adviser pastes a portfolio summary into a consumer AI assistant to draft a rebalancing rationale. A junior analyst asks a chatbot to summarize a prospect's estate documents. A partner runs an AI notetaker that joins client calls, transcribes them, and stores the transcript in a vendor cloud nobody evaluated.

Two problems compound. Data is the first: client financial detail leaving a governed environment for a system with its own retention and training terms. Records are the second. Whether a given prompt, draft or transcript is a required record is a question for your compliance officer and counsel, and it depends on the facts. The practical failure comes earlier: nobody can answer the question, because the record sits where the archive cannot see it. An archive that captures email and Teams but not the tools where drafting actually occurs has a hole in the middle of it.

Prohibition alone is usually unenforceable. Substitution works better: provide a governed AI capability inside the tenant where data boundaries, retention and audit logging already apply, then make the sanctioned path the easier one. Copilot governance is a question of whether permissions and sensitivity labels are accurate enough that an assistant summarizing everything a person can reach does not surface the compensation spreadsheet.

The familiar voice on the phone is no longer evidence

Synthetic voice has become an ordinary product feature rather than a research demonstration. The same SEC priorities name deepfake voice and video used for social engineering among the threats firms should be prepared for. That retires a verification method many firms still rely on informally: a recognizable voice on the line requesting a distribution, with urgency and a plausible story attached.

The control is old and now non-negotiable. Out-of-band callback to a number already on file, never a number the caller supplies, for every disbursement change and every first-time payee, with no exception for the client of twenty years who finds the question tedious. Document the callback. That documentation is simultaneously the fraud control and the evidence of it.

An opinion, offered as one: the firms most exposed here are those whose service culture treats friction as failure. Verification applied uniformly is a client protection, and saying so out loud converts an irritation into a demonstration of competence.

Four questions that do the work of a fifty-item checklist

The useful self-assessment for an advisory firm is four demonstrations, each of which should be possible inside a day.

  • Inventory. Produce a current list of every system holding client data and who has administrative access to each.
  • Restore. Show that a restore from backup was tested, by whom, and on what date.
  • AI and records. Name the AI tools your people actually use, and show whether what they generate falls inside your retention boundary.
  • Callback. Show that verification was performed and documented for every money movement, without exception.

A firm that can do all four cleanly is in better condition than one with a thicker binder and no artifacts. A firm that cannot has not necessarily uncovered a security problem. It has discovered that its evidence lives in individual people's memories, where no examiner, underwriter, or institutional allocator can use it. Start with whichever of the four would take longest.
