SOC 2 Readiness Is an Operations Problem

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  October 31, 2026

The sharpest question an enterprise buyer asks about your SOC 2 report is not whether you have one. It is which subservice organizations you carved out of the description, and what the report expects your customer to do about them. Put that question to a leadership team and watch what happens: the room turns toward whoever managed the audit project, and that person reaches for the PDF. The reflex is the finding. It tells you the report was procured as a sales artifact to clear a gate, rather than written as a description of how the company actually runs.

That approach held up for years. It is holding up less well now, and not because of any new regulation.

What the auditor actually signs

No company is SOC 2 certified. SOC 2 is an attestation engagement under the AICPA's trust services criteria, and the object of the examination is a document you write yourself — the description of your system. The auditor forms an opinion on whether that description is fairly presented and whether the controls you selected operated effectively over a stated period. Security is the required category. Availability, confidentiality, processing integrity, and privacy are elected.

Read that sequence again, because the business implication is easy to miss. A clean Type II opinion says you did what you said you would do. It does not say the product is well engineered, the architecture is sound, or the company is hard to breach. Two organizations holding reports that look identical from the outside can carry very different risk, and the difference lives in the scope section rather than the opinion letter.

What follows is analysis rather than established fact. Automated evidence-collection platforms have compressed the labor of producing a clean report. When a signal gets cheaper to produce, it carries less information, and buyers respond by reading underneath it — the exception list, the systems excluded from scope, the complementary user entity controls, the carve-outs. The report still opens the door. It no longer ends the conversation.

The subprocessor list is now an AI governance document

Here is what is genuinely different about 2026. Software companies have been shipping AI features at speed, and a large share of those features call a model hosted by someone else. The moment customer data crosses that boundary, the model provider is operating inside your system whether or not your description says so.

A capable security reviewer now asks specific questions. Does customer data leave your environment to reach a model provider. Is it retained there, and for how long. Do the contractual terms exclude it from training. Was that provider named in your subprocessor list, and did customers receive notice when you added it. Does change management cover model version changes and system prompt changes, or only application code.

Readiness programs designed before the AI layer existed tend to have no answer, because the controls were written for a product that did not yet make outbound inference calls. Running alongside that is the shadow version of the same problem. An engineer pasting production records into a consumer chat tool is both a control failure and a disclosure failure, and it surfaces in an audit interview rather than on a dashboard. Sound cybersecurity practice here is not a prohibition memo, because people route around those. It is a sanctioned tool with enterprise data terms, identity-based access, and a log that shows which path people actually took.

Most of the evidence is ordinary IT operations with receipts

Executives tend to picture SOC 2 as a specialized compliance discipline. Open the test procedures and that impression collapses. Access provisioning with documented approval. Offboarding inside a defined window. Multi-factor authentication and conditional access enforced across the identity estate. Privileged access reviewed rather than assumed. Log collection, plus evidence that a human looked. Change management with a reviewer who is not the author. Vendor reviews on a schedule. Restore testing that produced a result instead of an intention.

A competent IT function already does all of it. What an audit adds is the requirement that a control operated every time across the observation window. Suppose a departing employee's Microsoft 365 account stays active for six weeks because the offboarding ticket was never opened. One lapse becomes an exception, and exceptions follow a company into every future buyer's review.

Which is why the unglamorous systems carry more audit weight than the security tooling does. The ticketing queue is the evidence of your approval workflow. Your help desk is where access requests either get documented or get handled as a favor in a direct message. A data backup program only functions as an availability control if someone performs a restore and records the result. Pro Link Systems has served Los Angeles businesses since 1999, from Woodland Hills, and this argument comes from that operational vantage point rather than a compliance one. Controls rarely fail because a company lacks security sophistication. They fail because the work was informal and depended on a person who remembered to do it.

Scope decisions in month one set the bill in month nine

Three choices drive most of the cost and nearly all of the regret.

  • Which categories you elect. Availability and privacy often get added because a salesperson heard a prospect mention them. Each election commits you to operating additional controls indefinitely, and nothing is harder to walk back than a category you can no longer support.
  • When the observation window opens. Starting the clock before offboarding, logging, and change approval are reliable buys a report full of documented exceptions. That is a worse position than having no report yet, because those exceptions are now written down for every future reviewer.
  • What you carve out. Carve-outs are legitimate and common. The credibility problem arises when you cannot explain one in plain language to a buyer's security team, and that explanation is always requested.

A practical sequence for a first-time company: run the intended controls for a full quarter as a rehearsal, measure how often each one actually fired, fix the ones that depended on a specific person's memory, and only then open the window. A Type I report is a reasonable bridge during that period, provided everyone understands it describes design at a point in time and tests nothing about operation.

A better question than when the report will be ready

The honest executive framing is this. SOC 2 is a forcing function that makes IT operations repeatable and documented, and the sales unblock is a byproduct of having done that work. Reverse the order and you buy a PDF while leaving the underlying operation exactly as fragile as it was, which guarantees the next cycle is the same scramble at the same price.

So stop asking your team when the report will be ready. Ask instead which of these controls would quietly stop working if the person who runs it took a month off, and whether anyone would notice before an auditor did. The answer is your real readiness assessment, and performing it costs nothing.
