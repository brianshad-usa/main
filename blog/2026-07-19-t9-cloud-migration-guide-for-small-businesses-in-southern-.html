Cloud Migration Guide for Small Businesses

Most migration problems are decided before data moves: what to move, in what order and how to reverse a failed step.

Cloud migration for small businesses: decisions to make first

A cloud migration moves applications, data or both from servers you run to services run by a provider. For a small business in Los Angeles or elsewhere in Southern California, the decisions that determine success are made before any data moves: what to move, what to leave, in what order, how to reverse a failed step, and who owns each task. This guide is for owners and IT leads planning a first migration, such as file servers to SharePoint and OneDrive, a line-of-business application to a hosted version, or a data room to a cloud platform. It uses Microsoft's published migration guidance as its backbone, and the principles apply to other providers.

Inventory what you have and what depends on what

List every application, database, file share and device that touches business data, with an owner for each, how critical it is, and what it connects to. Microsoft's guidance on planning a migration puts dependency discovery first: components that must communicate with low latency should move together, and applications that share a database or sign-in service belong in the same group. Also record the constraints that make a move harder: software tied to a specific server, licensing that does not allow hosting, a legal reason to keep data in a particular place, and a line of business that stops if the internet connection fails. For each system, note how long the business could manage without it, because that sets the acceptable downtime during the move.

Choose a path for each workload

Not everything should move the same way, and some things should not move at all. The terms below are common in migration planning; the decision for each system is a trade-off between speed, cost and long-term benefit.

Migration approaches and when each fits
ApproachWhat it meansBest when
RetireSwitch off a system nobody needsUsage has dropped to nothing, or the data is archived elsewhere
Keep for nowLeave on site and revisitLegal, licensing or technical constraints block a move, or the hardware is fresh
Rehost ("lift and shift")Move as is, with minimal changesYou need speed, or the application cannot be changed
ReplatformMove with limited changes to use managed servicesA small change removes a recurring burden, such as managing a database server
Replace or refactorAdopt a hosted product or rebuild for the cloudThe current application is a limiting factor and the business can fund the change

For many small businesses the biggest, simplest gain is replacing a file server and an on-premises mail system with Microsoft 365 or a comparable service. That is a replace decision, not a lift and shift, and it comes with its own configuration work.

Sequence the moves and plan the way back

Microsoft's planning guidance recommends starting with simpler, lower-risk workloads, moving non-production environments first, and scheduling critical systems for later waves after the team has shown it can migrate successfully. It also suggests including one or two representative complex workloads in early waves, and avoiding critical business periods such as financial close or holiday peaks. It distinguishes between migrating with planned downtime, which is simpler and suits workloads that tolerate an outage, and near-zero-downtime migration, which needs continuous replication and more testing.

The part most small projects skip is the rollback plan. Microsoft's guidance is to define what counts as a failed deployment, write workload-specific rollback steps, test them, and secure stakeholder approval before starting. In practice, agree with the business in advance on the go or no-go criteria, such as sign-in working for all staff and a named person confirming the key application, and who has authority to reverse the change.

Security and responsibility change, but do not disappear

After a move, the provider secures the infrastructure and you remain responsible for data, accounts and access. Microsoft's shared responsibility documentation states that customers always retain responsibility for their data, endpoints, accounts and access management, including multi-factor authentication and conditional access, whichever service model they use. Plan these items as part of the project rather than after it: multi-factor authentication for every account, a small number of administrators, controlled sharing, and logs. Make sure cloud data is included in a backup you can restore; CISA's ransomware guide recommends keeping encrypted backups offline and testing restoration. See backup and recovery and cybersecurity for how this is usually set up. If you handle regulated data, ask your attorney or compliance adviser what the move requires before you start, not after.

A project outline you can use

  1. Inventory and owners. List systems, data, dependencies and a named owner for each.
  2. Decide the path. Choose retire, keep, rehost, replatform or replace for each system, with a short reason.
  3. Group and sequence. Put dependent systems in the same wave; start with low-risk ones and test with a pilot group.
  4. Write the rollback plan. Define failure, who decides, and how to return to the previous state.
  5. Prepare people and security. Set up identity, MFA, permissions and backup before cutover, and tell staff what changes and when.
  6. Cut over and watch. Move during a quiet period, keep the old system available for a stated period, and monitor performance and support requests.
  7. Optimize and govern. Review cost and usage monthly, remove unused resources, and set rules for who can create new ones.

How to judge the result

Set measures before the move: help-desk tickets in the first month, time to open the key application, number of unplanned outages, and monthly cost against the estimate you approved. Review them at thirty and ninety days. Cloud billing is usage-based for many services, so set a budget alert and assign someone to look at the bill each month, or costs will drift. A managed IT services provider or your IT help desk can carry out the work, but the business should hold the decisions about scope, timing and acceptable risk, and keep the documentation.
