Who Can Search Your Meeting Transcripts

AI meeting assistants turned candid speech into permanent searchable text. Most companies never decided how long to keep it, or who is allowed to search it.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 1, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. Transcription stopped being an event and became a default
  2. Search is what turned a quiet archive into an exposure
  3. Treat the meeting bot as an account, not a convenience
  4. Deletion is now distributed, and it collides with immutability
  5. The decisions worth making before the next quarter of meetings

Ask whoever runs your IT how long the company keeps meeting transcripts. The answer is often a clarifying question about which system you mean, and that pause is the subject of this essay. It is written for the executives, counsel and IT leaders who will have to answer for the archive. The decision it informs is how long machine-generated notes may live, and who may search them.

Many organizations adopted AI meeting assistants the way they adopt anything genuinely useful: quietly, from the bottom up, one enthusiastic manager at a time. The productivity case is real, and nobody misses taking notes. While the assistants were saving everyone that effort, though, they were also doing something structural. Candid, unedited business conversation became indexed, searchable, durable text. The policy, where there is one, tended to come later.

Transcription stopped being an event and became a default

Recording used to require an announcement. There was a button, a notification, a moment of friction, and a slight change in how people spoke afterward. That friction was doing governance work nobody ever credited it for.

Now an assistant joins, a transcript is generated, a summary is mailed to attendees, and action items are extracted into a task system. One meeting produces several durable artifacts instead of one person's handwritten notes. Multiply that across every recurring standup, one-on-one, pipeline review and vendor call, and a mid-sized company accumulates an archive of verbatim internal speech it would never have set out to build deliberately.

The defaults do the accumulating. In Microsoft Teams, a recording goes to the organizer's OneDrive or the channel's SharePoint site, with its transcript, where people invited from inside the company can view it and Microsoft 365 search indexes it. Under the default policy, using Copilot in a meeting requires a saved transcript, and the organizer cannot change that. Recordings and transcripts expire after 120 days unless an administrator sets something else. Expired files then move to the recycle bin, which holds work items for 93 days by default, so 120 days is closer to seven months.

This next point is analysis rather than fact: your retention schedule was designed for records people wrote on purpose. Contracts, reports, invoices, email. Writing carries a built-in editing pass, because people choose their words when they know the words persist.

Speech does not work that way. In a meeting, people speculate. Someone characterizes a customer bluntly, thinks out loud about a personnel problem, floats a price they will abandon four minutes later, or half-remembers an obligation incorrectly. All of it used to evaporate. Now it is a text file with a timestamp and a speaker label, governed, if governed at all, by whatever default the vendor shipped.

Search is what turned a quiet archive into an exposure

Video recordings sat in folders for years without causing much anxiety, for an unglamorous reason: nobody could find anything in them. Retrieving one useful sentence meant knowing which meeting, then scrubbing through an hour of it. The cost of retrieval was itself a control.

That control is gone. Transcripts are text, text is indexable, and the assistants layered across Microsoft 365 and comparable platforms exist to answer natural-language questions across what a user is permitted to see. Retrieval cost fell to near zero for legitimate users. It fell by the same amount for anyone holding their accounts.

Which produces a consequence that is easy to miss: in an AI-search environment, permissions become retention. If a transcript sits somewhere most of the company can open, an assistant can summarize and quote it for anyone who asks a plausible question. Oversharing that was tolerable when it required someone to go hunting is a live disclosure path once the hunting is conversational, instant, and performed by a tool that never gets bored.

The same logic serves an attacker inside a compromised account. It also serves a subpoena. Opinion, plainly stated: most organizations would far rather produce years of email in a legal proceeding than years of verbatim internal speech, and it is better to weigh the difference before the letter arrives.

Treat the meeting bot as an account, not a convenience

Two exposures deserve separate treatment, because they are governed differently.

  • An employee's personal assistant. Someone signs up for a note-taker with a personal account and brings it into internal meetings. The transcript lands in a vendor tenant you do not administer, under terms you have not read, tied to an identity that outlives the employee's departure. It is shadow AI in its purest form, and rarely malicious. Usually it is someone trying to be organized.
  • Someone else's assistant in your meeting. A vendor, a prospect or outside counsel brings a bot into a call where you discuss roadmap, pricing floor or security posture. You have disclosed to a third party's AI platform under an agreement you are not party to.

In California both have a legal edge. Penal Code section 632 makes it a crime to intentionally record a confidential communication without the consent of all parties. Whether a given bot, notice and meeting fall inside that rule is a question for counsel, and the answer should exist before the call does.

The useful shift is to stop thinking of these bots as features. A meeting assistant joins under some identity, consumes data and writes output somewhere. For governance purposes that is an account. It belongs in the same cybersecurity conversation as service accounts, API keys and the other non-human identities quietly operating in your environment. Ask what it authenticates as, what scope it holds, who approved it, and what becomes of its stored data when the human who invited it leaves.

Deletion is now distributed, and it collides with immutability

Suppose you decide a sensitive transcript should not exist. Deleting it is no longer one action.

The transcript sits in the meeting platform. Summaries sit in a dozen inboxes. Extracted tasks sit in a project tool. A copy may sit with a third-party vendor. Someone pasted the key paragraph into a chat thread. All of it may also sit in your data backup, where immutability is a property you deliberately paid for so that ransomware could not erase your history.

Notice the collision. Retention policy pushes toward disposal. Resilience architecture pushes toward indestructibility. Both are right within their own domain, and the tension between them is less a problem to solve than a boundary to draw on purpose, by someone with the authority to draw it. A legal hold overrides the schedule in either case, which is one more reason for counsel to be in the room. When nobody owns that boundary, the operative policy becomes keep everything, everywhere, indefinitely, which no board would ratify if it were ever written down.

The decisions worth making before the next quarter of meetings

None of this is an argument against AI note-takers. They are useful, they are not going away, and in our view banning them produces shadow adoption. It is an argument for a small number of decisions made deliberately, at an executive level, before an investigation forces them.

Six decisions, who makes them, and where they take effect (Microsoft Teams as the example)
DecisionWho makes itWhere it takes effect
How long transcripts, summaries and extracted tasks live, as their own record class. Shorter than instinct suggests, subject to legal holds and any record-keeping rule that binds youCounsel with the records ownerThe expiration setting in the meeting policy. It applies to new files and leaves existing ones alone.
Which meetings are never transcribed: legal strategy, HR investigations, board sessions, M&A, incident responseChief executive and counselA policy that lets organizers set Copilot to Off, which also stops recording and transcription
Whether Copilot may help without leaving a transcriptHead of IT with counselThe "Only during the meeting" mode, which does not save the speech-to-text data. Prompts may still be kept under a retention policy.
Which outside assistants may joinHead of IT or securityThe external-bot policy. Approval when detected is the default, and blocking is an option; Microsoft says some bots may go undetected.
Who can open and search the resultsThe owner of each site or mailboxFile permissions, corrected before AI search is widened
What participants are toldCounsel and HRThe invitation, the written policy, and the Teams setting that requires explicit consent to recording

The last row is the cheapest. Behavior in meetings changes when participants know what persists, and telling them costs almost nothing.

Many companies will keep using AI note-takers. Fewer will decide, on purpose, how long those notes are allowed to live. That decision, more than the technology, is what separates a productivity gain from a liability assembled one meeting at a time.
