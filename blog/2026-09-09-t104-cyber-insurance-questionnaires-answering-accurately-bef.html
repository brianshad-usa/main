Your Cyber Insurance Form Is an Audit

A cyber insurance application is a set of representations. Measure each answer first, disclose the exceptions, and have counsel review the wording.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 9, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The form is a set of representations, and the signature says so
  2. Current questions ask for scope, not inventory
  3. Part of your answer is visible from outside your network
  4. The qualified yes is the professional answer
  5. What the exception list is actually worth

Key takeaways

  • Questionnaire answers are representations the carrier relies on; an inaccurate one can give it grounds to contest coverage.
  • Current forms ask about scope: which accounts, what share of devices, and when a full restore was last tested.
  • Start a quarter early, measure each answer from a system of record, and disclose exceptions as a qualified yes.

The most consequential technical document your company files this year may be a four-page insurance questionnaire signed by an officer who did not fill it out.

The sequence is familiar. A broker sends the renewal application six weeks out. Someone forwards it to IT, or to the managed service provider, or to whoever handled it last year. Answers come back as a column of checkboxes. A CFO or COO signs, the policy binds, and nobody reads the document again until there is a claim, at which point it becomes one of the first things the carrier reads.

That asymmetry is the problem. The person with signature authority may not know what the questions are measuring, and whoever answers them may not register that the answers function as representations the insurer relies on. This article is written for both of them.

The form is a set of representations, and the signature says so

What follows is analysis rather than legal advice; have your broker and coverage counsel test it against your own policy language and your state's law. An insurance application is a set of representations a carrier relies on when deciding whether to issue a policy and at what price. The forms do not hide this. One large carrier's multi-factor authentication supplement has an executive officer represent, to the best of their knowledge and after reasonable inquiry, that the answers are true and complete and may be relied on as the basis for providing insurance. It commits the applicant to report material changes, and it asks for confirmation that the signer had help from the person in charge of IT security. Another carrier's cyber application states that it will form part of the policy if coverage is bound.

Where a material representation proves inaccurate, an insurer may contest coverage and, in some circumstances, seek to rescind the policy as though it had never existed. California law does not make intent the test. Under the Insurance Code, concealment of a material fact, "whether intentional or unintentional," entitles the injured party to rescind, and materiality turns on the effect the truth would have had on the insurer's decision. In October 2026 a California appellate court applied those rules in a published opinion about an aircraft policy, holding that the duty to disclose a material fact applies even where the carrier never asked about it. How far that reasoning reaches a cyber application, and whether the decision stands, are questions for counsel. The principle is not new. What is new is how much technical detail now sits inside the representations.

Picture the mechanics. Your application says multifactor authentication (MFA) is enforced on all remote access. Ten months later, a business email compromise moves money out of an operating account through a legacy service account that was excluded from Conditional Access because a line-of-business application broke when MFA was applied to it. Nobody lied. The exception was documented in a ticket. But the attested answer and the operating reality do not match, and that mismatch hands the carrier an argument it would not otherwise have.

Premium is not the exposure. The exposure is discovering, during the worst week of your company's year, that the instrument you bought to transfer risk is being disputed instead of paid.

Current questions ask for scope, not inventory

Older applications asked whether you had antivirus, a firewall, and backups. Inventory questions are easy to answer honestly, because owning a thing is binary. Scope is where real environments diverge from the mental model on the org chart. Wording varies by carrier; in the two forms linked above, the pattern runs this way:

  • Identity, with qualifiers. Not whether MFA exists, but whether it is required for email, for all remote access by employees, contractors and third parties, and separately for administrative access to directory services, backup environments, network infrastructure, endpoints and servers. One form also asks which type of MFA is in use.
  • Endpoint coverage as a fraction. Whether endpoint detection and response (EDR) is on every endpoint, and if not, which assets lack it and what mitigates the gap. Servers are where the exclusions tend to hide.
  • Backup properties rather than backup existence. Whether copies are encrypted, immutable or kept off the network, whether reaching them requires MFA, and how long essential functions would take to restore.
  • Privileged access. Whether MFA protects all local and remote access to privileged accounts, a question that leads straight to the service accounts and automation credentials that cannot perform MFA at all.
  • Third parties and payments. Which vendors are most critical, and whether a change in payment instructions is verified by calling a number that was already on file.

The emphasis matches what regulators hear from the market. In 2025 a cyber insurance provider told a National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) working group that, from an underwriting perspective, four controls come first: MFA, employee training, patch cadence and backup hygiene.

Offered as a prediction rather than a settled market standard: AI usage questions will expand across the next two renewal cycles. Expect carriers to ask which AI tools staff may use with company data, whether that use is governed by written policy, and whether generative tools are connected to internal file stores. Anyone pricing data-exfiltration risk cares whether employees paste client records into unsanctioned tools, and whether an assistant in a Microsoft 365 tenant inherits permissions nobody has reviewed.

Answered from memory, all of these questions produce generous numbers.

Part of your answer is visible from outside your network

A questionnaire used to be the only window a carrier had into your environment. Exposed remote desktop, unpatched edge appliances, expired certificates, permissive or missing email authentication records, credentials from your domain circulating in breach dumps: all of that is observable without touching your network. Some carriers say so in writing. The second application above has the applicant consent to non-intrusive scans of its internet-facing systems, and the NAIC session described outside risk signals read alongside the paper form. Whether a specific carrier examines a specific account is not something to assume in either direction. Assume that some do, and write your answers as though yours is one of them.

Which makes an inaccurate answer more than a claim-time problem. It is a credibility problem at underwriting.

The qualified yes is the professional answer

The instinct is to answer cleanly. That instinct is expensive.

A qualified yes (enforced on all administrative and remote access, three documented legacy service accounts still excluded, compensating controls listed in an attached addendum, remediation scheduled and dated) is more defensible than a bare yes nobody verified. The forms make room for it: both provide a space to explain any "No." Our view is that disclosed exceptions read as program maturity rather than weakness. Treat that as opinion, not a promise about your renewal, and have your broker and counsel review the wording before it is submitted.

The change worth making is a calendar change. Begin the questionnaire a full quarter before the deadline and treat the first pass as a measurement exercise rather than a form-filling exercise. For each question, ask who measured this, when, and from what system of record.

Measure before you attest
Question areaSystem of recordWhat to confirm first
MFA scopeConditional Access export and sign-in logsEvery exclusion by name, including vendors and emergency access accounts
Administrative accessDirectory roles, backup console, network devicesWho holds standing rights, and which consoles sit outside central sign-in
Endpoint coverageEDR console set against the device inventoryServers and unmanaged devices missing from the console
BackupsRestore test record and backup platform settingsThe date and scope of the last full restore, not the last successful job. Only one of them is disaster recovery
Third-party accessEnterprise applications, remote access tools, vendor listPersistent connections, and who approved each
External exposurePublic DNS and an external scanEmail authentication records, exposed remote access, edge device firmware

Where nobody can answer, the unknown is itself the finding. A quarter also leaves room to close the small gaps. Missing enforcement on a handful of accounts, an unpatched edge device, standing administrative rights that should have converted to just-in-time access months ago: several of those are weeks of work rather than quarters, and fixing them before you attest is better than having to disclose them.

What the exception list is actually worth

Here is the reframe worth carrying into the next renewal. For a mid-market company too small to face a formal regulatory audit, the insurance questionnaire may be the most detailed outside look its controls receive all year, and it arrives attached to a document you were going to sign anyway. The exception list produced by answering honestly makes a useful cybersecurity roadmap, because every item on it is something an organization that pays claims thought worth asking about. That is a judgment about where the value sits, not a claim about your carrier.

For the executive who signs: read the questions before you sign the answers. You do not have to evaluate the controls yourself. Ask one question of whoever filled the form in, what system of record did this come from, and decline to sign until the answer is something other than a shrug.
