What Cyber Insurers Actually Verify Now

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  November 07, 2026

A cyber claim is decided twice. Once when the incident happens. And once about a year earlier, when someone at your company signed the renewal application and attested that multi-factor authentication was enforced on all remote access, that backups were segregated and tested, that privileged accounts were kept separate from daily-use accounts.

The second decision is the one most executives never revisit. It is also the one that shapes how the first one goes.

Most organizations treat the application as procurement friction: a form IT fills out, the broker shops, the CFO signs. A more accurate reading is that you are making specific factual representations about the operating state of your business, in writing, to a counterparty whose claims team will read them closely at the worst possible moment. What follows is analysis, drawn from how these applications themselves have changed rather than from a cited study, and it is not legal or insurance advice. Your broker and counsel own the policy language. The operating implications belong to management.

The questionnaire stopped being self-reported

Cyber underwriting used to run largely on trust. You described your controls, the carrier priced the risk, and verification was rare. The ransomware loss years ended the patience for that posture.

Carriers now commonly look at what is visible from outside your network — exposed remote access, unpatched edge devices, email authentication records, credentials circulating from prior breaches — and increasingly ask for evidence rather than assertion. A tenant configuration export. The conditional access policy as configured. The date of the last restore test and the name of the person who ran it.

The practical consequence for a 50- or 300-person business in Los Angeles is that the distance between what leadership believes is true and what is actually configured has become expensive. That distance is almost always real. MFA is enforced for staff but excluded for the three executives who objected. A service account running a line-of-business integration has a password set years ago and never rotated. Legacy authentication is still permitted because an old scanner needs it. None of this is negligence. It is the ordinary residue of a business that grew faster than its managed IT practices.

The attestation does not record why the exception exists. It records that you said there was none.

Carriers now price your identity system, not your firewall

Put a 2026 application next to one from 2019 and the shift is unmistakable. The firewall section has shrunk. The identity section has multiplied.

Carriers want to know whether administrative accounts are separate from user accounts. Whether privileged access is time-bound or standing. Whether your Microsoft 365 tenant enforces conditional access by device compliance and location, or merely asks for a second factor that a phishing proxy can relay in real time. Whether endpoint detection has a human watching it, or an antivirus product writing alerts nobody reads at 2 a.m.

The logic is not complicated. An attacker who signs in with a valid credential does not trigger the controls designed to catch an attacker who breaks in. That is the shape of risk underwriters have adjusted to, and they have adjusted faster than many of the businesses they insure.

One asymmetry deserves naming. Identity controls are among the least expensive serious security work available to a mid-sized business — largely configuration of licensing you already pay for — and they are also the controls carriers weigh most heavily. A focused quarter of IT security work on identity shows up in both your risk posture and your renewal conversation. Few line items do both.

The headline limit is rarely the limit for your likeliest loss

Cyber policies are bundles of distinct coverages with distinct limits. Ransomware response, forensics, business interruption, breach notification and regulatory defense typically draw against the headline limit on the declarations page. Social engineering and funds-transfer fraud — an employee deceived into wiring money to an attacker — frequently sit under a separate sublimit that is a fraction of that number, and in some policies exist only by endorsement. This is structural rather than exotic, and it is worth confirming on your own declarations page instead of assuming in either direction.

Then consider which event is more probable at a professional services firm, a nonprofit, or a construction company in greater Los Angeles. Business email compromise requires no malware and no encryption. It requires one convincing message about a changed bank account, arriving during an escrow closing or a payroll run. The confirmation call that used to settle the question is also less reassuring now that a convincing voice can be synthesized from publicly available audio.

So the renewal question is not how much cyber coverage you carry. It is this: what is the limit for the loss we are most likely to suffer, and does that coverage depend on a procedure we actually follow? Funds-transfer coverage is commonly conditioned on documented out-of-band verification of payment changes. If finance verifies by replying to the email thread, the step that would have earned the payout is the step that was skipped.

Backup questions are restore questions in disguise

Applications ask whether backups are immutable, segregated and tested. Those three words do more work than they appear to.

  • Immutable means an attacker holding domain administrator rights cannot delete or encrypt the backup.
  • Segregated means the backup console does not authenticate against the directory the attacker just compromised.
  • Tested means someone restored a meaningful workload recently and measured how long it took — not that the nightly job reported success.

The third is where attestations most often outrun reality. A data backup that completes reliably and restores slowly is still a business interruption measured in days. Underwriters have begun asking for recovery time objectives instead of backup schedules, which is the better question and a harder one to answer honestly.

Read the application as an operating specification

The reframe worth carrying into your next renewal is this. The insurance application is the most specific, externally enforced description of required operating controls your business is likely to receive. It was written by people who pay for failures across thousands of companies. Used well, it is a roadmap. Signed carelessly, it is a liability you manufactured yourself.

Three items belong on an executive calendar rather than an IT backlog:

  • Reconcile the signed application against the actual configuration at least once between renewals, line by line, with evidence attached.
  • Surface every exception granted for convenience, and decide at leadership level whether to close it or disclose it.
  • Confirm that the loss you are most likely to suffer is covered at a limit that matters, under conditions you can prove you met.
