5 Signs Your Los Angeles Business Has Outgrown Your Current IT Provider

Five signs, each with a measure from your own records, and a scorecard for the conversation with your IT provider.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
June 3, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
4 min read
In this article
  1. Sign 1: The provider reacts instead of preventing
  2. Sign 2: Response times no longer match the cost of downtime
  3. Sign 3: No one is advising on decisions
  4. Sign 4: Security has not kept pace with the business
  5. Sign 5: You cannot see or verify what you pay for

Five signs your Los Angeles business has outgrown its IT provider can each be checked with evidence, which matters because dissatisfaction with IT is common and often has a cause other than the provider. This article is for owners and operations leaders who suspect their current IT support no longer fits and want a fair test before deciding to stay, renegotiate or switch. For each sign there is a measure you can take from your own records, and the final section covers what to do if the evidence points toward changing.

Sign 1: The provider reacts instead of preventing

If nearly every contact begins with an outage, the provider is working from your problems instead of from a plan. Look for the preventive work in your records: patch reports, backup test results, a replacement schedule for aging hardware, and a list of open risks. If none of that exists, ask whether it is outside the contract or simply not being done. A provider that monitors systems should be able to show you what it found and fixed before you noticed.

Sign 2: Response times no longer match the cost of downtime

A few hours of waiting may have been acceptable at ten people and is not at forty. Pull the last three months of tickets and calculate the time to first response and the time to resolution, and note how many were reopened. Compare that with what the contract promises and what a lost hour now costs you. Also check what happens after hours: who answers, and how fast.

Sign 3: No one is advising on decisions

As the business grows, decisions such as a new office, an acquisition, a move to the cloud or a line-of-business replacement need technical input before money is committed. If your provider only closes tickets and has never raised a recommendation, you are making those decisions alone. The test is practical: when did they last present a recommendation with options, costs and a timeline? A provider that cannot do this may not be wrong, but the role may have to come from elsewhere, such as an internal lead or a separate advisor.

Sign 4: Security has not kept pace with the business

More staff, more customer data and more systems mean more to protect. Check the basics against the CISA ransomware guide, which calls for phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, offline encrypted backups that are regularly tested, and a written incident response plan approved by the chief executive. If any of those are missing, ask when they will be in place and what it costs. Industry rules, such as those covering health or financial data, add obligations on you that a provider cannot take over; ask counsel what applies. For a security baseline, see cybersecurity and backup and disaster recovery.

Sign 5: You cannot see or verify what you pay for

Reports are the evidence that the work is happening. Ask whether you receive a monthly summary of tickets, patching, backups and open risks, and whether you hold current documentation: an asset list, a network diagram, and administrator credentials stored where the company can reach them. If the provider holds all of this and cannot or will not share it, switching later becomes costly, which is a reason to fix it now whatever you decide.

What to do with the evidence

Start by sending the provider the scorecard and asking for a written plan with dates. Many gaps are scope problems that can be fixed with a revised agreement, and a provider that responds well to a clear request may be worth keeping. If the answers are weak, plan the switch as a project: obtain your documentation and credentials first, compare at least two managed IT services proposals on the same scope, agree an overlap period, and check the notice terms in your current contract. Switching costs time and carries risk of its own, so the evidence should justify it. Judge any provider, old or new, after ninety days: reports delivered, response targets met and a restore test passed.
