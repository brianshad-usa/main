HIPAA Compliance Checklist for LA Practices

A HIPAA Security Rule checklist for Los Angeles medical practices, written as questions to work through with counsel.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
July 11, 2026
3 min read
In this article
  1. Administrative Safeguards: Policies and Procedures
  2. Technical Safeguards: Securing Patient Data
  3. Physical Safeguards: Protecting Your Facility
  4. Documentation and Ongoing Review

For medical practices across Los Angeles, HIPAA is a regulatory requirement and a responsibility to protect patient trust. This checklist summarizes common Security Rule topics so practices can identify gaps and discuss them with their attorney or a qualified HIPAA assessor. It is general information, not legal advice, and it does not tell you what your practice is required to do.

Administrative Safeguards: Policies and Procedures

The rule requires a designated security official responsible for developing and implementing security policies. Many practices also name a privacy official; in a small practice they may be the same person.

Common administrative items include:

  • Risk Assessment: Conduct a security risk analysis to identify vulnerabilities in how your practice handles Protected Health Information (PHI). The rule does not set a frequency; repeating it when systems change and on a regular schedule is common practice.
  • Staff Training: The rule requires a security awareness and training program for all workforce members. Keep records of training.
  • Business Associate Agreements: Have signed Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) with vendors that handle PHI for you, including your managed IT services provider, billing companies and cloud service providers.
  • Incident Response Plan: Document how you will respond to security incidents. For breach notification, individuals must be told without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery; ask your attorney about other notices.

Technical Safeguards: Securing Patient Data

Technical safeguards are the technology-based protections for electronic PHI (ePHI). Many practices use experienced IT support providers to implement them.

Common technical items include:

  • Access Controls: Unique user IDs for each person. Multi-factor authentication is not named in the current rule but is widely used. Automatic logoff is an addressable specification, meaning you implement it or document an alternative.
  • Encryption: Encryption of ePHI is an addressable specification; most practices encrypt laptops, phones, backups and email that carries PHI, or document their reasoning.
  • Audit Controls: Systems that record and examine activity in systems containing ePHI, with someone assigned to review the records.
  • Data Backup and Recovery: Secure backups of ePHI with tested restores. Your disaster recovery plan should consider how patient care continues during outages.

Technical controls such as these are part of a practice's cybersecurity program, and they do not by themselves make a practice compliant.

Physical Safeguards: Protecting Your Facility

Physical measures are as important as technical ones. Your Los Angeles medical practice must control physical access to facilities and equipment containing ePHI.

Common physical items include:

  • Facility Access Controls: Badges, visitor logs or cameras to control who enters areas where ePHI is stored or accessed, with stricter controls for server rooms and records storage.
  • Workstation Security: Monitors positioned away from patient and visitor views, privacy screens where needed, and workstations in public areas that lock when unattended.
  • Device and Media Controls: Policies for secure disposal of devices and media containing ePHI, including wiping or destroying hard drives and shredding paper records.
  • Mobile Device Management: If staff access ePHI on phones or tablets, use management tools that allow remote wiping if a device is lost or stolen.

Documentation and Ongoing Review

Security is ongoing, and documentation shows your efforts during audits or investigations. Keep:

  • Written policies and procedures
  • Records of staff training
  • Risk assessment reports and remediation plans
  • Signed Business Associate Agreements
  • Incident reports and breach notifications
  • Access logs and security incident investigations

The rule requires required documentation to be retained for six years from creation or the date it was last in effect, whichever is later. See healthcare IT services for the technical side, and work with your attorney or a qualified assessor on the legal determinations.
