Ransomware Protection for LA Law Firms

A law firm's ransomware risk is shaped by client confidentiality; this covers recovery objectives, backup design that survives an attack and the first hours of an incident.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
July 23, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
5 min read
In this article

  2. Prevention: the controls that carry the most weight
  3. Backups: design for recovery, then test it

Ransomware protection for Los Angeles law firms differs from protection for other small businesses in one respect: the data that is encrypted or stolen belongs to clients and is covered by duties of confidentiality, so an incident is both an operational crisis and a professional-responsibility event. This article is for managing partners and firm administrators who must decide how much downtime the firm can tolerate, how backups are designed and who does what in the first hours of an attack.

Why law firms are exposed, and what is not known

Firms hold client communications, deal and litigation files, and financial records, and they run on deadlines such as filing dates and closings. Those features give an attacker leverage: the firm cannot easily stop working, and it cannot tolerate disclosure of client files. That is an analysis of the incentives, and it is not a ranking of industries; no verified source is cited here for how often law firms are targeted compared with other sectors, and size does not protect a firm because most intrusions begin with a stolen password or a malicious email rather than with a choice of victim.

The firm's own ethical position also matters. California lawyers have duties of competence and confidentiality that extend to reasonable steps to protect client information, and the State Bar of California has published ethics guidance on data breaches. The firm's general counsel or ethics counsel, not its IT provider, should state what those duties require in an incident, including when clients must be told.

Prevention: the controls that carry the most weight

CISA's #StopRansomware Guide recommends phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication on email and remote access, and advises escalating to senior management any system that cannot enforce it. For a firm, the first review is simple: list every account that can reach the document management system, email and remote access, and confirm each requires MFA. Add these:

  • Email filtering and a reporting button. Staff who report a suspicious message quickly give IT time to remove it from every mailbox.
  • Least privilege. Not every timekeeper needs access to every matter. Ethical walls in document management are a security control as well.
  • Separate administrator accounts that are not used for email and web browsing.
  • Patching and supported software, including servers and the firewall. Windows 10 reached end of support on October 14, 2025, so any computer still on it needs an upgrade or Microsoft's paid Extended Security Updates program.
  • Network segmentation so that one infected workstation cannot reach the file server and the backup system.

Backups: design for recovery, then test it

The same CISA guide recommends offline, encrypted backups of critical data and regular testing of their availability and integrity, because many ransomware variants search for backups to delete or encrypt. The widely used 3-2-1 rule (three copies, two media types, one off-site) is a sensible starting point, with the addition that at least one copy cannot be changed or deleted from the firm's network, whether by being offline or by immutable storage.

Cloud services do not remove the need for a backup. Microsoft's description of data resiliency in Microsoft 365 says the service is built to survive its own failures, and it notes that most data loss results from customer actions, which is why it provides ways for customers to restore accidentally deleted items. Ransomware that encrypts synchronized files or an attacker who deletes a mailbox are customer-side events. Ask what recovery window your Microsoft 365 setup provides and whether a separate backup is justified.

Set two numbers with leadership before choosing technology. The recovery time objective (RTO) is how long the firm can be without its systems; the recovery point objective (RPO) is how much recent work it can afford to lose. A firm with court deadlines might choose an RTO measured in hours and an RPO of an hour or less, and then ask the provider to prove the backups meet it with a timed restore of the document management system.

The first hours of an incident

CISA's guide includes an incident response checklist. The steps below follow its sequence, adapted for a firm.

  1. Isolate. Disconnect affected computers from the network and identify which systems are involved. Do not wipe them yet; evidence may be needed.
  2. Convene the decision group. Managing partner, ethics or general counsel, the IT provider and the cyber insurer's hotline, if the firm has a policy. Read the policy before engaging outside responders, because some policies require using the insurer's approved responders.
  3. Report. The guide advises reporting to CISA, the FBI or the Internet Crime Complaint Center, and consulting law enforcement about possible decryption tools before any decision on payment.
  4. Notify as required. Counsel decides what clients, insurers and regulators must be told and when. California's breach reporting page states that a business notifying more than 500 residents must send the Attorney General a sample notice.
  5. Recover and review. Restore from offline, encrypted backups, reset passwords, and document what happened and what changed.

Cyber liability insurance deserves its own review: which incident costs are covered (response, restoration, business interruption), what security controls the policy requires, and whether answers on the application match what is configured.

Choosing IT security support for a Los Angeles law firm

Ask for proof rather than descriptions: the MFA coverage report, a timed restore from the last test, the incident plan and who is called first. Ask how the provider is reached at 7 a.m. before a hearing. Judge results by the number of accounts without MFA, the measured restore time against your RTO, the number of reported phishing messages, and unsupported systems still in service.
