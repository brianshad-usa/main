IT Support for Century City High-Rises

A Century City tower adds building constraints and, for financial firms, rules the firm owns. What to settle with the landlord, counsel and your IT provider.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
July 29, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
4 min read
In this article
  1. Building questions that decide your network
  2. Which rules may apply to a financial firm
  3. Business continuity for a short-notice outage
  4. What to ask a managed IT provider

IT support for Century City high-rise offices has two layers that rarely appear in the same conversation: the building, which you share with other tenants and do not control, and the regulations that apply to financial firms, which you cannot delegate. This article is for office managers, chief operating officers and compliance officers at financial services and professional firms in Century City towers. It explains which building questions to settle before a lease is signed or a system is changed, and which rules to confirm with counsel. It is analysis, not legal advice.

Building questions that decide your network

In a multi-tenant tower, the constraints that cause delays are usually physical and contractual. They are cheaper to learn about before you commit to a suite than after.

  • Which internet carriers already have fiber into the building, and which would need landlord approval and new conduit? Get a written install date from each carrier, not a verbal estimate.
  • Can you reach the riser, the telecom room or your own suite's demarcation point without the landlord escorting you, and what notice does building management require?
  • Is there a second physical path for connectivity, or does every carrier share one entrance? A cut or a building-wide outage then takes out both.
  • What are the rules for after-hours access, freight elevator use and contractor insurance? These set the schedule for any equipment change.
  • Is there power conditioning or backup power for tenant suites, and what happens to your equipment during building maintenance shutdowns?
  • How crowded is the wireless spectrum on your floor? In a tower, many neighboring networks compete for the same channels, so ask for a site survey rather than relying on the number of access points.

Plan for the lease-end move as well. If your cabling and network design are documented, the next move, or the next provider, costs less. Help desk staff who have been in the building before will know which riser, carrier and building contact to start with; ask any provider whether they have, and who on their team does.

Which rules may apply to a financial firm

Which regulation applies depends on what kind of firm you are, so the first task is to ask your compliance officer or counsel to name them. Two federal rules are commonly relevant to firms in this district, and both put a duty on the firm itself that an IT provider can support but not take over.

Two federal rules to ask counsel about
RuleWho it may coverWhat it asks of the firm
FTC Safeguards RuleNon-bank financial institutions under FTC jurisdiction, such as mortgage brokers, tax preparers and some advisersA written information security program with a named qualified individual, a written risk assessment, access controls, encryption, multi-factor authentication, oversight of service providers, an incident response plan, and notice to the FTC within 30 days of discovering a breach affecting 500 or more consumers
SEC Regulation S-P (as amended)Broker-dealers, registered investment advisers, investment companies, funding portals and transfer agentsAn incident response program, and notice to affected individuals as soon as practicable and no later than 30 days after becoming aware of an incident

The FTC rule applies to institutions not subject to another regulator's enforcement under the same statute, so a firm covered by the SEC rule is usually outside it. Both rules require the firm to oversee its service providers, which includes its IT provider. That oversight is a practical request: ask your provider for the written list of who has administrator access to your systems, how that access is protected with multi-factor authentication, and how quickly they would tell you about an incident. The SEC's phase-in periods for the amended rule (18 months for larger firms, 24 for smaller ones, counted from Federal Register publication) have now run, so assume the amended rule applies and confirm with counsel.

Business continuity for a short-notice outage

Tower outages tend to be building-wide and out of your hands, so the useful question is how fast the firm can work somewhere else. Decide who must be able to work remotely on the day, test it with a short drill, and confirm that email, file access and the phone system do not depend on equipment inside the suite alone. Files that live in cloud services with multi-factor authentication make that drill easier; a server in a closet makes it harder. Keep backups verified by restore, not just completed, and have a written incident plan that names who calls counsel and who calls clients.

What to ask a managed IT provider

Compare providers on response targets, after-hours coverage, and on-site arrival time to your building. For reference, Pro Link Systems is based in Woodland Hills and includes same-day on-site support when needed in its managed plans; ask every provider what their written commitment is and how it is measured. Judge a provider by evidence you can check each quarter: restore test results, a current access list, patch status, and the time from ticket to first response.
