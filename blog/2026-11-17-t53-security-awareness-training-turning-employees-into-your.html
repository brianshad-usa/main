Train Staff to Verify, Not to Detect

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  November 17, 2026

For more than a decade, security awareness training taught employees a dependable checklist. Look for misspelled words and clumsy grammar. Be suspicious of a generic greeting, an odd sender address, or a subject line built on panic. The checklist worked because, for most of that period, fraudulent messages were cheap to produce, and it showed.

That assumption has expired. A language model can draft a fluent message in a colleague's register in seconds. Voice-cloning tools can imitate a familiar voice from recordings that are often publicly available. The tells employees were drilled to catch have largely disappeared. Many training programs still teach people to look for them.

For an executive, the useful question has moved. Whether to train staff is settled. What matters in 2026 is what training should accomplish when the fake is indistinguishable from the real thing, because the answer changes how the program is designed, measured, and paid for.

Why a polished message is now the dangerous one

Detection-based training rests on a premise: fraudulent messages contain mistakes, and an attentive employee will notice them. Generative AI removed the mistakes. Attackers can also feed a model what most companies publish freely, including leadership bios, press releases, vendor announcements, and job postings. What comes back references real projects and real people.

Our analysis, not a measured finding: this makes old-style training counterproductive in one narrow way. An employee taught that scams look sloppy also learns the inverse, that a polished, specific, well-timed request must be legitimate. That is precisely the message a capable attacker now sends.

The same logic reaches the phone. Suppose a voice sounds exactly like the CFO, names the right vendor, and knows the quarter-end deadline. Careful listening will not settle whether it is real. In our view, human perception is the wrong instrument for this job, and asking employees to become better instruments is a losing strategy.

Train the procedure, not the instinct

A 2026 program trains employees to follow a rule rather than make a judgment. The question shifts from "is this real?" to "what does our process require before I act?" That second question has an answer even when the first does not.

In practice, that means a short list of non-negotiable verification rules. Each rule is tied to an action an attacker actually wants someone to take:

  • Payment and banking changes are confirmed by calling a number already on file, never one supplied in the request.
  • Urgent executive requests involving money, credentials, or sensitive data are confirmed on a second, independent channel.
  • Password resets and MFA changes require identity checks at the help desk that a confident caller cannot talk past.
  • Unusual requests by phone or video can be paused and called back, with no penalty for the delay.

The strength of this approach is easy to miss. An employee who follows the callback rule defeats a flawless deepfake without ever suspecting it was one. Nobody had to detect anything; the procedure did the work.

One condition makes or breaks it: executives have to follow the rules too, and visibly. A verification policy collapses the first time a senior leader skips a callback because it felt inconvenient. Employees learn which rules are real by watching who is exempt. Attackers impersonate executives precisely because people hesitate to slow them down. A program that ignores that hesitation is training around the actual attack.

Measure how fast people report, not how often they click

Most awareness programs lead with one metric: the share of employees who clicked a simulated phishing link. It is easy to collect and easy to chart, and it measures the wrong thing. Click rates describe failure. We would argue they can also reward fear, because employees who are embarrassed or disciplined for clicking learn to stay quiet next time.

The more useful signal is reporting: how many people flag a suspicious message, and how quickly. An early report gives whoever monitors email security a chance to find and remove the same message from other inboxes before more recipients act on it. In Microsoft 365, the built-in Report button in Outlook puts that action one click away. Making sure someone is watching what comes through it is the organizational work.

A few principles follow:

  • Treat an employee who clicked but reported within minutes as a success, not a failure.
  • Thank people for false alarms. A team that reports ten harmless messages is the team that reports the eleventh.
  • Track time-to-report alongside participation, and review both with leadership like any other operating metric.

Let identity controls carry what training cannot

The phrase "first line of defense" deserves skepticism. If a single click or phone call can move money or expose the company, the problem is architecture, and no training program will fix it. Employees are one layer among several, and the controls around them should make their mistakes survivable.

That means phishing-resistant sign-in, such as passkeys, so a convincing fake login page has nothing reusable to capture. Conditional Access policies should weigh device, location, and sign-in risk before granting access. Approval rights for payments and vendor record changes should be narrow, and those changes should trigger alerts. Together, controls like these make a successful phish far more likely to end as an inconvenience than as an incident.

Training then has a sharper job: covering the attacks no technical control can see. Business email compromise and voice fraud often involve no malware and no malicious link, only a persuasive request to a person with authority to act. That residual risk is where human procedure matters most, and where awareness budgets should concentrate.

Three questions to ask before renewing your training contract

Awareness training is still worth funding, but its purpose has changed. Teaching people to spot fakes is no longer the goal. The goal is teaching them what to do when they cannot. Before the next renewal, ask your security team or provider three things:

  • Which verification rules exist for payments, credential changes, and executive requests, and are executives bound by them in practice?
  • What are our reporting rate and time-to-report, and who acts on a report when it arrives?
  • Which accounts still sign in without phishing-resistant authentication?

The second question has an edge that is easy to overlook: reporting only works if a human answers. An employee who gets a convincing call at 8 p.m. needs someone to tell right then, not a queue that opens in the morning. What happens after that call matters just as much. A report should set off a response plan that was written down before anyone needed it.
