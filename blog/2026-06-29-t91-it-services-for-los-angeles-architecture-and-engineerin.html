IT Services for Architecture & Engineering

AEC firms need IT built around large shared models; this compares hosting options, explains restore testing on real projects and lists what to ask a provider.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
June 29, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
4 min read
In this article
  1. What makes design workloads different
  2. Where project models live: three common designs
  3. Protecting designs, drawings and client information
  4. Support that fits design work and how to judge it
  5. Choosing a provider for a Los Angeles design firm

IT services for Los Angeles architecture and engineering firms are built around a different constraint than most offices: very large design files that many people edit at once, on workstations that cost a great deal and take a day to rebuild. This article is for principals and operations leads of architecture, engineering and construction (AEC) firms who must decide where project models live, how people reach them from home or a site, and how to protect both the files and the client information inside them.

What makes design workloads different

Revit, AutoCAD, Civil 3D and Navisworks users notice problems that others do not: a project model that takes minutes to open, a sync that stalls, a render that ties up a workstation. These problems usually come from one of four places: the workstation specification, the network between workstation and file location, the storage holding the models, or the licensing and plug-in versions across the team. A provider that treats them all as "slow computer" tickets will not fix them. Ask for measurements: model open time, sync time, and what changed after each fix.

Workstation age has a new deadline. Windows 10 reached end of support on October 14, 2025. Microsoft's paid Extended Security Updates program is available for commercial devices, but it covers security updates only and does not include technical support. Before moving design workstations to Windows 11, confirm that the vendor supports your software versions and plug-ins on it, and test one project on one machine first. This is advice from experience, not a vendor rule.

Where project models live: three common designs

Comparing ways to host project models
DesignWorks well whenWatch for
On-premises file or model serverMost staff are in one office and the network is fastRemote staff are slow over VPN; the server and its backup become a single point of failure
Cloud-based project collaboration platformMany consultants and outside parties share modelsWho holds the master copy, how long the vendor retains it, and whether you keep your own backup
Virtual or cloud-hosted workstationsStaff work from home or travel, and models are too heavy to moveCost of graphics-capable machines, session quality, and a user experience you must test before committing

Many firms use a mix. The decision for leadership is which copy is the one of record, and who is responsible for backing it up when part of the project is in a platform owned by someone else.

Protecting designs, drawings and client information

A firm's intellectual property is its drawings and calculations, and its liability includes whatever confidential client or facility information they contain. Three practical points deserve attention. First, outside parties: consultants, contractors and clients should have named accounts with access limited to the folders they need and an end date, rather than shared links that stay open. Second, contracts: owners, public agencies and prime contractors increasingly attach security terms to projects. Read them before signing, and where a contract refers to federal controlled unclassified information (CUI), involve counsel. NIST's small business pages on protecting CUI describe the relevant guidance (NIST SP 800-171), which is a federal standard for protecting CUI. Third, ransomware recovery: CISA's #StopRansomware Guide recommends offline, encrypted backups and regular restore testing, because ransomware commonly tries to delete reachable backups.

For an AEC firm, test the restore on a real project, not a document folder. Open the restored model in the design application and confirm that links to external references, families and sheets still resolve. Pro Link Systems tests backups by restoring them; ask any provider to show you the same result, with the time it took.

Support that fits design work and how to judge it

A help desk is useful for a designer only if it understands the applications well enough to separate a hardware fault from a license or plug-in problem. Ask how the provider handles a failed workstation on deadline day, whether it keeps a ready spare, and how quickly a rebuilt machine returns to a working state. Ask for support hours that match project deadlines rather than the standard workday.

Measure the arrangement with a few numbers: average model open and sync times, hours lost to workstation failure, the time to rebuild a machine, the result of the latest restore test, and the number of outside accounts that have passed their end date. If the provider cannot report them, add that to the contract before renewing.

Choosing a provider for a Los Angeles design firm

Local presence helps when a workstation needs hands-on repair, and a provider based in the region can usually reach you sooner; ask what the commitment is in writing. It does not replace knowledge of your applications. Ask for a reference from another design or engineering firm, and for how the provider would handle your largest current project: where it would live, how it would be backed up, and how a consultant on another continent would gain access safely.
