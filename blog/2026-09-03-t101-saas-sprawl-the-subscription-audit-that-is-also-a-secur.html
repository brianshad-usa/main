Canceling SaaS Doesn't Revoke Access

Canceling a SaaS subscription ends the invoice, not the OAuth grant. The subscription audit that finds wasted spend also finds access grants nobody revoked.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 3, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. Cancellation Ends the Invoice, Not the Grant
  2. The Tool You Approved Three Years Ago Reads More Than It Did
  3. Finance Has the Best Software Inventory in the Building, and It Is Blind in One Direction
  4. Build a Register, Not a Cancellation List
  5. Count Owners, Not Logos

Somewhere in your Microsoft or Google tenant is an application with read access to a shared mailbox. An administrator who left two years ago approved it, for a tool your finance team canceled at the following renewal. The seat is gone. Billing stopped. The permission did not.

That gap between commercial termination and technical termination is the part of SaaS sprawl nobody owns. Finance treats subscriptions as spend. IT treats applications as access. Both read the same list from opposite ends, and the middle is where the interesting problems live.

A useful reframe for 2026: a subscription audit and an access audit are the same exercise performed in different vocabulary. Run either one properly and most of the other falls out as a byproduct. Many mid-sized companies run neither, because the spending version feels like a cost-cutting chore and the access version has no budget line. This article is for the CFO and the IT lead who would have to run it together.

Cancellation Ends the Invoice, Not the Grant

Worth stating plainly, because this is a fact about how modern authorization works rather than an opinion about any vendor: when someone connects a third-party application to your identity provider, the resulting consent lives in your directory, not in the vendor's billing system. Ending the subscription removes seats. It does not remove the enterprise application, the permissions attached to it, or the keys the vendor still holds. Tokens age out only when nobody uses them; in the Microsoft identity platform a refresh token lasts ninety days by default and is replaced on every use, so an integration the vendor leaves running renews itself.

Who approved the application decides what happens when people leave. Where one employee granted access to their own mailbox, disabling that employee's account cuts off the application's access to it. Where an administrator consented for the whole organization, or granted permissions that let the application act as itself, nobody's departure changes anything.

The same holds for the connective tissue that accumulates around any tool that was genuinely useful:

  • API keys minted for the departing tool and pasted into systems that are still running
  • Inbound webhooks and automation flows that still fire
  • Service accounts with delegated mailbox or file rights, exempted from multi-factor authentication (MFA) years ago "temporarily"
  • DNS records pointing a subdomain at infrastructure the vendor may have since recycled, which opens the way to subdomain takeover
  • SFTP or file-share credentials shared during onboarding and never rotated

When a person leaves, someone disables an account, because a checklist says so. When a tool leaves, someone stops paying, and in many companies that is the whole ritual. This is not negligence. Nobody was ever assigned the second job.

The Tool You Approved Three Years Ago Reads More Than It Did

Something changed recently enough that many of your approval decisions predate it. Business software has been adding embedded assistants, summarizers, and "chat with your data" features at speed. No number is needed to make the point, and none should be invented: open five tools your team uses and count the ones that gained an assistant after you bought them.

The consequence is analysis rather than a reported finding, and it is the part executives underestimate. Your original consent decision was made against a product description that no longer applies. A tool approved as a scheduling utility now reads meeting content to produce summaries. File-transfer services index what passes through them. Where the initial grant was broad, covering all mail, all files or the directory, nobody had to ask you again. Nothing was violated. The product simply grew into permissions you had already given it.

Shadow AI sharpens the picture. Employees are not exfiltrating data maliciously; they paste a contract into whichever assistant renders it readable fastest. Companies that have invested seriously in Microsoft 365 governance and Copilot data controls sometimes find the harder problem sits outside that boundary entirely, in a free-tier tool signed up for with a work identity, which cost nothing and therefore appears in no ledger finance maintains.

The review question has moved. It used to be what does this application do. Now it is what can this application read, what does it do with what it reads, and where does that end up. Those are IT security questions wearing a procurement costume.

Finance Has the Best Software Inventory in the Building, and It Is Blind in One Direction

Card and accounts payable data is often the closest thing a 20-to-500 seat business has to a real software asset inventory. It is also systematically blind in exactly the wrong direction. Paid tools appear. Free tiers, expired trials whose integration quietly survived, personal-card purchases reimbursed as office expense, and self-serve signups authorized with a corporate identity do not. Those last ones can hold broad permissions, because one-click sign-in is how free products reduce signup friction.

Know your defaults before you start. Microsoft's managed consent policy, the default for new tenants, now routes mail, calendar, file and Teams chat requests to an administrator; older tenants may differ, and grants made earlier remain. In Google Workspace the starting position for third-party apps an administrator has not configured is to let users access them, until someone changes it.

So the audit worth running reconciles three ledgers that rarely agree:

  • What finance pays for: the spend view, complete on cost, blind on access
  • What your identity provider has authorized: the enterprise applications and consent grants, complete on access, silent on cost
  • What your network and browsers actually talk to: the behavioral view, which catches what neither of the others knows exists

Findings live in the disagreements.

Reading the three ledgers against each other
What you seeWhat it usually meansFirst action
Authorized in the directory, no invoiceA free tool nobody vetted, or a canceled tool nobody disconnectedFind an owner; disable if none comes forward
Invoice, no sign-in activityMoney leaving for nothingConfirm with the owner, cancel, then disconnect
Network or browser traffic onlyShadow IT that bypasses both recordsIdentify the data involved before the person
Service account or key with no tool behind itThe remains of an integrationRotate, watch for breakage, remove
All three agreeA governed applicationSet the next review at renewal

Machine identities belong in the same reconciliation. Service accounts, API keys, and automation connectors have no leaver's date and no manager, which is precisely why they survive the humans who created them.

Build a Register, Not a Cancellation List

A cancellation list saves money once. A register changes how decisions get made. For each application that survives review, capture the business owner by name, the data classification it touches, whether authentication runs through single sign-on or a local password, how many people hold admin rights, what it can read inside your tenant, the steps required to disconnect it, and how you would get your data out if the vendor disappeared. That last field is where SaaS sprawl meets disaster recovery: a platform holding business records you cannot export is a continuity exposure regardless of the vendor's uptime.

Use renewal dates as the governance clock; every renewal is a scheduled, budget-backed excuse to re-examine scope. Then put the disconnect procedure with whoever runs your IT support function, because that group sees a tool's entire lifecycle: the request, the integration, the tickets, the abandonment. The steps are short.

  1. Disable sign-in for the application. In Entra ID this stops all new tokens and can be reversed, so it is the safe first move.
  2. Wait one business cycle. Month-end is when forgotten integrations announce themselves.
  3. Remove the permissions and then the application, user grants included.
  4. Rotate what the vendor held: API keys, shared passwords, SFTP credentials, webhook secrets.
  5. Delete the leftovers: DNS records, mail-flow exceptions, MFA exemptions, firewall rules.
  6. Export, then get deletion confirmed in writing, and close the register entry with a date.

The consent side of the register, including which permissions deserve the closest reading, is in The Access That Survives a Password Reset. If your company has recently completed a cyber insurance renewal questionnaire or a large customer's vendor review, you have likely met a version of these questions already, under time pressure.

Count Owners, Not Logos

Consolidation is the reflexive answer and only sometimes the right one. Collapsing a dozen tools into one platform trades vendor sprawl for concentration risk, which is a different exposure rather than a smaller one. The honest goal is fewer unowned applications: every tool attached to a named person who can say what it reads and how it would be removed.

A company with sixty governed applications is in better shape than one with fifteen where four hold permissions nobody can explain. That is opinion, but it gives you the measure to report: the number of authorized applications with no owner. The subscription review is the least intimidating door into a serious access conversation, because it opens on the CFO's turf.
