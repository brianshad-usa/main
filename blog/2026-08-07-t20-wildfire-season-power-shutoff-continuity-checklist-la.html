Power Shutoff Continuity Checklist for LA

Power shutoffs usually come with warning, but not always. Three checks turn a Southern California continuity plan from written into tested.

Pro Link SystemsInsights

Having a continuity plan and having run one are different things. To be ready for power shutoff season, a Los Angeles-area business usually does not need a bigger plan. It needs a tested one, and testing it takes an afternoon. This checklist is for the owner or operations lead who has to settle, before the next wind event, who makes which call.

Ninety seconds on the three checks. Watch it on YouTube, or take the written version with you — the SoCal Business Continuity Checklist is free and needs no email address.

A Power Shutoff Is Usually Announced. Plan for When It Is Not.

A public safety power shutoff (PSPS) is a utility turning off power to reduce the risk that its equipment starts a fire. What makes it difficult is that it is outside your control and lasts as long as it lasts. Southern California Edison says it begins sending notifications up to three days ahead, sends a final notice one to four hours before when possible, and may give little or no notice when conditions change fast. That last clause is not theoretical. A state Assembly oversight paper reports that in Edison's January 2-17, 2025 event, 31 percent of the accounts shut off received no 24-to-48-hour notice.

Which utility serves the building matters as much as the weather. Edison serves much of the region around the city. Inside the City of Los Angeles the supplier is the Department of Water and Power, whose September 2026 wildfire plan says it is still evaluating a proactive shutoff protocol. LADWP can cut power in response to a fire or a specific threat, and says some notifications may follow the outage. Look up which utility you have, and check its policy again each season.

Either way, the failure mode is paralysis rather than panic. Staff arrive at the office. The phones do not work. The files are somewhere, technically. Two people stand near a dark conference room display trying to decide whether to send everyone home, and neither is certain who gets to make that call. Nothing about that morning is dramatic. It is simply an expensive, avoidable day.

What "Tested" Actually Means

When we say a plan has been tested, we do not mean somebody read it. We mean the organization has answered three questions out loud, with names attached, on a day when it did not matter.

1. Sequence: What Comes Back First?

Not everything has to come back at once. Plans tend to imagine restoration as a single event, and then on the day it turns out half the systems depend on the other half and nobody wrote down the order. Decide it in advance. For most businesses it is some version of:

  • Identity first, because almost nothing else authenticates without it. NIST's recovery guide makes the same point.
  • Communications second, because you cannot coordinate a recovery you cannot talk about.
  • Revenue systems third: the one or two things that actually bill, ship or serve customers.
  • Everything else, in whatever order is convenient.

One dependency deserves a direct question. If staff sign in to Microsoft 365 through servers in your office, by federation or pass-through authentication, a shutoff there can stop cloud sign-in too. Microsoft recommends enabling password hash synchronization as a backup. With pass-through authentication the switch is manual, so it belongs in the rehearsal.

Your order may look different, and that is fine. The point is that an order exists, it is written down, and it is not being invented at 7 a.m. in a dark office.

2. Location: Where Does the Copy Live?

A backup that shares a failure domain with the thing it is backing up is a second copy of the same risk.

The test is a single question: if this building loses power for three days, can you still reach the copy? If the answer involves walking into that building, you have your answer.

This is more often an unexamined problem than a difficult one, because the backup job runs, it reports success every night, and success reports are reassuring right up to the moment they become irrelevant.

3. Rehearsal: Has Anyone Actually Run It?

Here is the uncomfortable one, and the step that gets skipped. Reading a plan tells you the plan exists. Running it tells you whether it works, and only that is useful on the day.

A rehearsal does not have to be elaborate. Emergency planners call the basic version a tabletop exercise: people talk through their roles in a particular emergency. Put four people in a room on an ordinary Tuesday. Say: the power is out at the main office, it is out until Thursday, go. Then watch what happens.

What you are looking for is not a smooth performance. You are looking for the pauses. Every pause is a decision nobody had made yet, and every one of them is cheaper to find on a Tuesday than on the day. They are seldom technical. They are questions like:

  • Who decides that we are working remotely?
  • Who tells clients, and what do they get told?
  • Does whoever answers the phones know what to say?
  • If the outage runs past Thursday, who makes the next call?

Those are not IT problems. They are the reason an IT plan does not survive contact with a real outage.

Why an Afternoon Is Worth It

Our view, offered as opinion: the businesses that come through a shutoff week well are often not the ones with the most sophisticated infrastructure. They are the ones where somebody spent an afternoon, once, being slightly bored in a conference room, arguing about what comes back first.

Put the date on the calendar before the windy season, keep the result on paper, and run it again when the phone system, the sign-in setup or the building changes. The disaster recovery planning guide covers how the same thinking applies to other outages, hardware failure and ransomware.
