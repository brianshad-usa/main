The Callback Proves Less Than It Did

Voice cloning weakened the verification callback. The durable fix is approval bound to identity and device, so a perfect impersonation still fails.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
August 15, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The thing that broke was independence, not authenticity
  2. Why "train them to hear the fake" is the wrong line item
  3. Authenticate the channel, not the speaker
  4. Five rules a finance team can run without a project plan
  5. The decision only the executive team can make

A vendor you have paid for six years sends updated remittance details. New bank, correct letterhead, an invoice number that matches your ledger. Your accounts payable lead follows the policy exactly: no reply to the email, no number taken from the letter. They pull the contact from your own records, dial the line they have used for years, and reach the controller they have spoken with a dozen times. Same voice. Same slight impatience. He confirms the change, the payment releases, and the money is gone.

That is a constructed scenario, not an incident report, and it depends on one more fact. A cloned voice cannot answer a line its owner still controls. In this story the intruder was already inside the vendor's cloud account, the vendor's phones ran on that same account, and the call was forwarded before anyone's desk rang. The control did not fail because someone was careless. It failed while being executed correctly.

This article is for the CFO and the controller who own the payment rule. The decision it informs is what your company will accept as proof that a payment instruction is genuine.

The thing that broke was independence, not authenticity

Verification never depended on any single check being unforgeable. It depended on the checks being independent, so that an attacker had to compromise two unrelated things at the same moment. Email could be spoofed, so you confirmed by phone. Phone numbers could be spoofed, so you dialed one you already held. Individually the controls were mediocre. In combination they worked, because holding all of them at once was expensive.

Two changes lowered that expense. The first is synthetic audio. The FBI has warned that criminals use AI-generated voices and real-time video to impersonate people their targets trust, and the sample a convincing voice needs is often one the executive published: a conference panel, a webinar recording, a podcast appearance, an outgoing voicemail greeting. The second is consolidation. Where phones run on the same cloud account as mail, one stolen session can reach both. In Microsoft Teams, for example, forwarding calls to an external number is allowed by default. A carrier talked into a SIM swap does the same to a mobile line.

An intruder reading a compromised mailbox already knows the invoice numbers, the tone of the relationship, the names on both sides, and the timing of the payment cycle. Add the phone line and your two independent factors were never really two. They were one intruder appearing twice. That is analysis rather than a measured claim, but the logic is hard to argue with.

Proportion matters here. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded more than $3.0 billion in business email compromise losses for 2025, and businesses reported over $30 million of that as involving AI. Most of this fraud still does not need a cloned voice. The case for changing the rule now is that the rule costs little to change and the callback is the step everything else leans on.

Why "train them to hear the fake" is the wrong line item

The reflexive response is awareness training: teach the finance team to listen for flat affect, odd pauses, breathing that does not match the sentence. Some of that had value when synthetic speech was cruder. Treating it as a control today is, in my view, a category error.

Consider what the ask actually is. You are requiring someone in the payment chain to perform real-time forensic audio analysis, over a compressed phone line, while a senior-sounding person applies time pressure. Then you make that judgment the last thing standing between your company and a wire. If the call goes wrong, the loss is the company's and the blame lands on the least empowered participant. That is not a control. It is a liability transfer.

There is a second problem, and this one is a prediction rather than a fact. The artifacts people are trained to notice are precisely the defects that model developers are working to eliminate. Any control whose effectiveness declines each quarter according to somebody else's product roadmap does not belong on your risk register as a mitigation. Moving the conversation to video does not rescue it: in November 2026 the FBI described a theft scheme built on fake meetings that play recorded webcam footage of real people, with deepfakes as a supplement.

Train your people, certainly, but train them on process authority rather than audio perception. The most valuable thing a finance employee can learn this year is that no voice, however familiar, has standing to change a payment instruction.

Authenticate the channel, not the speaker

The reframe worth taking to your controller this quarter is simple to state. Stop trying to authenticate the human on the line. Authenticate the channel the approval arrives through, and choose a channel an outsider cannot occupy even with a perfect impersonation.

What each check proves, and what defeats it
CheckWhat it provesWhat defeats it
Familiar voice on an inbound callThat someone sounds rightVoice cloning; spoofed caller ID
Callback to a number in the requestNothingThe requester chose the number
Callback to a number recorded before the requestThat someone can answer that line todayCall forwarding, a SIM swap, or an altered vendor record
Live videoThat the picture looks rightRecorded or generated video
Pre-shared phraseThat the speaker knows a secretA phrase stored in the compromised mailbox
Approval from an enrolled device with phishing-resistant sign-inThat a specific credential and device approved this transactionCompromise of that device, or an exception made around it

Practically, that means moving payment approvals out of voice and email and into a system where identity is proven cryptographically rather than acoustically. Much of that machinery already sits in the identity layer of Microsoft 365. A Conditional Access policy, which requires Entra ID P1 licensing and managed devices, can demand phishing-resistant sign-in and a compliant device before the approval application opens. A cloned voice cannot produce that approval. The attacker can imitate your CFO flawlessly and still lack the CFO's credential and registered device. Where money is released in a bank portal your identity provider does not control, ask the bank for hardware-token or passkey sign-in and a second approver.

This is the same shift that reshaped the rest of cybersecurity over the past several years. Identity became the perimeter because everything softer than identity stopped being dependable. Finance workflow is one of the last places the older assumptions survived, protected by the comfortable belief that you know what your colleagues sound like.

Voice keeps one honest use: a pre-shared verification phrase, agreed in person or through an authenticated channel, rotated on a schedule, kept out of email, and never spoken on an inbound call the other party initiated. The FBI gives families the same advice. It works because the secret is independent of the voice.

Five rules a finance team can run without a project plan

My recommendation is a short set of written rules. None of them is a regulatory requirement. The companion piece on wire fraud from real inboxes covers the mailbox side and what to do once money has moved.

  • Bank detail changes are never authorized in a single conversation. Any change to a payee's account triggers a mandatory hold and a confirmation that your team initiates through a contact route recorded before the request existed, preferably to a second person at the payee.
  • Urgency raises scrutiny. Policy should say plainly that time pressure from a senior person increases verification rather than reducing it, and that nobody will be penalized for the resulting delay.
  • Approvals live in an identity-bound system. Above a defined threshold, sign-off comes from a compliant device with phishing-resistant authentication. Phone calls, chat messages and forwarded emails do not count.
  • Friction follows counterparty risk as well as dollar amount. A first payment to a new account deserves more scrutiny than a routine payment to a long-standing vendor, whatever the size.
  • Ask your insurance broker, in writing, how your policy responds when an employee is deceived into authorizing a transfer, and how that differs from a response to an intrusion. Treat the answer as a design input for the rules above rather than a footnote you read after an incident.

Test it: pull the last three bank-detail changes and ask what evidence approved each one. If any answer is a phone call or an email, the rule is not yet in force.

The decision only the executive team can make

None of this starts as a technology purchase. It is a decision about authority: specifically, a decision that senior people surrender the ability to move money by sounding like themselves. Executives who bypass the process are the vulnerability, because every exception they demand becomes the script an attacker reuses. When the chief executive can override a control with a phone call, the control does not exist. It merely has good manners.

My judgment is that the version of this that holds is the one where the CFO writes the rule and is visibly the first person bound by it. Configuration follows that. Conditional Access policies, approval workflows and a help desk that answers when something looks wrong are all solvable problems. The governance decision is the one that has to be made.
