AI Policy Should Govern Data, Not Tools

AI policies name vendors, but the risk lives in features. Govern data classes, enforce fewer rules, and make the sanctioned path faster than the shortcut.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
August 29, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The approved-tools list stopped describing reality
  2. A rule nobody can observe is a suggestion
  3. Shadow AI is a latency problem first
  4. Write for data classes, then give people amnesty
  5. What to demand from the next draft

Key takeaways

  • AI now arrives as features inside software you already license, so approved-tool lists decay; govern classes of data instead.
  • Sort every rule into technically enforced or socially agreed. A rule nobody can observe is only a suggestion.
  • Make the sanctioned route faster than the shortcut, and give staff a no-blame way to report mistakes.

Executives will tell you their company has an AI policy. Fewer can say when anyone last opened it. The document is usually fine: drafted with care, circulated, acknowledged in the HR portal, filed. What went wrong is not the writing. The policy was built to govern a world that has quietly stopped existing, and nobody scheduled a moment to notice.

That is a design problem rather than a discipline problem, and it is worth understanding before you commission another draft. This essay is for whoever signs the policy. The decision it informs is what the next version should govern: a list of tools, or the data those tools touch.

The approved-tools list stopped describing reality

The usual AI acceptable-use policy has one structure: a list of sanctioned tools, a list of prohibited ones, and a paragraph telling employees not to put confidential information into either. That structure rests on an assumption, that AI is a destination, a website someone consciously decides to visit.

It is not only that anymore. AI now arrives as a feature inside software your company already licenses: in the browser, in the meeting recorder, in the CRM sidebar, in the PDF reader, in the note-taking app someone installed on a personal phone, in the transcription service that joins the call uninvited. Capability appears by vendor update rather than by procurement decision. An employee can pass company information through several AI systems before lunch without once making a choice about AI.

This is the structural reason approved-tools lists decay. The list names vendors. The risk lives in features, and often in the account behind them, because the same product can carry different terms depending on how it was bought. OpenAI, for one, says it may train on content from individual accounts unless the user opts out, and does not train on business accounts by default. A policy that says "ChatGPT is approved" has not yet said enough.

Analysis, not prediction: the useful unit of governance has moved from the tool to the data. A policy organized around what may leave the building will outlive a policy organized around which logos are permitted.

A rule nobody can observe is a suggestion

There is a test that separates a working policy from a decorative one. Take each rule in the document and ask a single question of it: if someone broke this, how would we know?

Many AI policies struggle to pass it. "Do not enter client information into public AI tools" is a reasonable sentence and an unobservable one, unless something in your environment is watching for it. Rules that cannot be observed are hopes with a signature block attached.

The remedy is not surveillance. It is honesty about which category each rule belongs to. Some rules are technically enforced: which AI features are on in your tenant, which outside apps can obtain consent to your data, which accounts require phishing-resistant sign-in. Others are socially agreed, because they are judgment calls. Mark every rule as one or the other, and write down how a breach of it would come to light:

Sorting the rules: enforced or agreed, and how you would know
RuleEnforced or agreedHow a breach comes to light
Client data stays out of consumer AI sitesEnforced on computers running endpoint data loss prevention, agreed everywhere elseSharing sensitive information with a listed AI site in the browser is warned or blocked
Copilot does not process our most sensitive filesEnforced by a data loss prevention policy tied to sensitivity labelsA test with a labeled file. Files a user uploads into a prompt are not scanned, so that part is agreed
No outside app reads mail or files without approvalEnforced by consent settingsThe request queue, and a quarterly read of the app list
A person checks AI output before a client sees it, and says so where the client's terms askAgreedSampling by the team lead
A request to move money is confirmed by calling a known numberAgreed, and it can be auditedA callback log. The FBI warns of AI-generated voices and real-time video of supposed executives in fraud

Both kinds are legitimate. Confusing them is what produces a document nobody follows, because employees work out quickly which sentences have teeth and mentally discard the rest, including the ones that mattered. If your enforced rules are few, the fix belongs in your Microsoft 365 configuration and your identity controls, not in a longer document. The same goes for what an assistant can reach. Copilot follows the permissions already on your files, so a badly shared folder becomes a badly answered prompt.

Shadow AI is a latency problem first

The standard explanation for unsanctioned AI use is that employees are careless or indifferent to security. In our reading that explanation is usually wrong, and it sends executives toward the wrong intervention.

People route around the sanctioned path when the sanctioned path is slower. Picture an analyst at 4:40 on a Thursday holding a contract she needs summarized before she leaves. She will use whatever returns an answer in ninety seconds. If the approved route requires a ticket, an approval, and a wait until tomorrow, she is not filing the ticket. She pastes the contract into whatever is already open in her browser and goes home.

Reframe the executive decision accordingly. Ask how fast the permitted route runs as well as what the policy forbids. Sanctioned capability that genuinely beats the shortcut does more for governance than any amount of policy language. Speed is a control.

The same logic explains why responsive IT support behaves as a security mechanism rather than a convenience line item. Someone who can get a real answer in minutes has no reason to invent a workaround. Someone who expects to wait a day builds a private toolchain, and you learn about it much later, usually from the wrong direction.

Write for data classes, then give people amnesty

A policy employees can apply names the data, not the vendor. Three classes are usually enough, and each needs an example drawn from the reader's real job rather than an abstraction.

  • Never leaves sanctioned systems. Client financials, patient information, case files, unreleased results, employee records, anything under contract or regulation. A medical practice would write this line around the patient information HIPAA protects, in its own language and with its own counsel. Passwords and keys belong in no prompt at all.
  • Sanctioned tools only. Internal documents, drafts, code, meeting content: proprietary but not regulated.
  • Open. Published material, marketing copy, general research questions.

Then add the clause that is easy to omit: a no-blame reporting route. Somebody will paste the wrong thing into the wrong window. What decides the cost is whether they tell you in ten minutes or ten weeks. Promising punishment for honest error guarantees silence, and silence is the expensive outcome. State plainly that self-reported mistakes are handled as containment rather than discipline, settle with HR and counsel where deliberate misuse begins, and then honor the promise the first time it is tested. That first case becomes the real policy no matter what the document says.

One further clause deserves a line, and it is the genuinely new one for 2026. AI agents increasingly act with a user's authority. Microsoft describes Copilot Cowork as carrying out tasks across Microsoft 365 on the user's behalf. Acceptable use now has to cover what an employee may authorize software to do in their name. That is an identity question, and it belongs with the rest of your cybersecurity program rather than in an HR annex.

What to demand from the next draft

An AI policy is an extension of identity and data governance. It should be owned by whoever owns those, and it needs a review date on the calendar, because the feature surface changes without consulting you. To check the draft for risks it has missed, compare it with NIST's Generative AI Profile, a companion to its voluntary AI Risk Management Framework. Then judge it by three things:

  • It fits on two pages.
  • Every rule is marked as technically enforced or as a judgment call, and each enforced rule names its control.
  • The permitted path is faster than the shortcut it replaces.

Fail the third test and the document loses to convenience.
