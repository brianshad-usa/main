IT Disaster Recovery Planning Guide for LA

A recovery plan answers in advance which systems return first, how fast and with how much data lost, then proves it with restore tests.

IT disaster recovery planning: what to decide and test

An IT disaster recovery plan answers one question in advance: after systems fail, in what order do you bring them back, how fast, and with how much data lost? This guide is for owners and operations leaders at Los Angeles businesses who need to set recovery priorities and prove that backups and procedures work. The causes are varied in Southern California, including earthquake damage, wildfire evacuations, utility power interruptions, ransomware and plain hardware failure, but the plan is organized around what the business cannot do without, not around the cause.

Rank systems by what the business can tolerate

Start with a business impact review. For each system, ask how long operations could continue without it and how much recent data you could afford to re-enter. Two terms capture this. The recovery time objective (RTO) is how quickly a system must be back. The recovery point objective (RPO) is how much data, measured in time, you can lose. The federal Ready.gov guidance on IT disaster recovery plans says to inventory hardware, software and data, identify critical applications and prioritize their restoration, and to match IT recovery times to the recovery objectives of the business functions that depend on them. Finance, not IT, usually has the best view of what an hour of downtime costs, so involve it. Keep the numbers as estimates you can defend rather than precise-looking figures with no basis.

Design backups for the failures you expect

A backup protects you only if it survives the event that damaged the original and can be restored in the time you need. Three decisions matter more than the product choice:

  • Separation. Keep at least one copy somewhere that a single event cannot reach: another region, another provider, or an offline copy. The CISA ransomware guide recommends offline, encrypted backups and notes that many ransomware variants try to find and delete or encrypt accessible backups.
  • Coverage. List what is not backed up: cloud mailboxes, shared files, line-of-business applications, laptops, configuration of firewalls and the identity system. Microsoft's shared responsibility documentation states that customers always retain responsibility for their data, so do not assume a cloud provider's built-in recycle bin or retention setting is a backup you control. Check the retention settings, and decide whether an independent backup is justified.
  • Restoration. A backup that has never been restored is an assumption. Pro Link Systems, for example, verifies backups by restore testing; whichever provider you use, ask to see the date and result of the last restore test.

Cloud-hosted recovery can help because it does not depend on your office building, and Southern California businesses that rely on one office should weigh that. It still depends on internet access, so decide how staff will connect if the office is closed or the network is down. See backup and recovery and cloud services for how this is typically structured.

Plan people and communication, not only technology

During an incident, the recurring failures are confusion and delay. Write down who declares a disaster, who contacts customers and staff, how people reach each other if email is down, and where the printed contact list and credentials are kept. Include vendor emergency numbers and the steps to move phone service. Decide in advance which tasks staff can do offline or from home. For regulated businesses, ask your attorney or compliance adviser what notification or record-keeping duties apply after a data loss.

Test on a schedule and record the result

Ready.gov's guidance is to test the plan periodically to make sure it works. How often is a judgement call that depends on how critical and how changeable your systems are. As a working rule, test restoring your most critical system at least once a year and after any major change, and test a file-level restore more often. Each test should leave a record.

  1. Pick a scenario. For example, the main file store is unavailable, or the office is closed for a week.
  2. Time a real restore. Restore from backup into a test location and compare the time and data age with your RTO and RPO.
  3. Check the people steps. Can the right person find credentials, the contact list and the vendor numbers?
  4. Write down the gaps. Record what took longer than planned, what was missing and who will fix it by when.
  5. Update the plan. Change the document, the backup or the objective, and set the next test date.

How to judge whether the plan is real

Ask four questions at least annually: when was the last successful restore, and how long did it take; what is the oldest data we would have lost; who is the named owner of each system; and what has changed in our environment since the plan was last updated. A managed IT services provider or your IT help desk can run the technical tests, but leadership should own the objectives and see the results.
