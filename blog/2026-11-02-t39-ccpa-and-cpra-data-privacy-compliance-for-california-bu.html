The CCPA Deletion Request You Can't Fill

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  November 02, 2026

A verified deletion request is the most honest audit your company will ever receive. It does not read your privacy policy. Nothing in how your notice at collection is worded changes the answer. One question matters: where, exactly, does this person's data live?

Most California businesses between 20 and 500 seats can answer that for the website. The marketing stack is known, the CRM is known. Everything after that is where trouble starts — the HR folder on the file server, resume attachments sitting in a hiring manager's mailbox, a spreadsheet a department head exported two years ago, the ticketing system, the archived mailbox of someone who left in 2022, the backup set, the handful of SaaS tools nobody in IT approved. A request that should take minutes becomes a weeks-long excavation. That distance between minutes and weeks is your real privacy posture.

The distance has grown, and not because the statute changed last month. It grew because of how data moves inside a company now.

Scope is a moving line, not a one-time check

The California Consumer Privacy Act, as amended by the California Privacy Rights Act, applies to businesses that meet defined thresholds — tied to annual revenue, to the volume of California residents whose personal information the business processes, and to revenue derived from selling or sharing that information. Most executives check those thresholds once, conclude they sit outside, and never revisit the question.

Two assumptions sit underneath that conclusion, and both deserve a second look with privacy counsel rather than from memory. The first is that the thresholds are fixed. They are defined in law and subject to change, so the figure you checked is not necessarily the figure that applies today. The second assumption is that the resident count tracks customers. It tracks personal information processed, which is a far wider net — email lists, web analytics, consumer-facing applications and a sizable workforce all contribute to it.

This is analysis, not legal advice, and it deliberately contains no figures. Do not take a threshold number from a blog post, including this one. The structural point stands without one: a compliance program resting on a threshold check performed three years ago rests on a number that may have moved underneath it.

The largest regulated dataset in your building is your own staff

The most consequential shift for mid-market California employers was not a new consumer right. It was the end of two carve-outs. Personal information about employees, applicants and contractors, along with information exchanged in business-to-business contexts, sat outside most CCPA obligations on a temporary basis, and that arrangement was not extended. Workforce and business-contact data now live inside the same framework as consumer data. Confirm the current treatment with counsel; the operational consequence is what should occupy you.

Picture a Los Angeles firm with two hundred employees. Its consumer-facing footprint might amount to a mailing list and a contact form. Its workforce footprint includes applications, background checks, benefits enrollment, payroll, performance documentation, disciplinary files, accommodation correspondence and years of email. That data is older, more sensitive, less inventoried, and stored precisely where nobody maps — departmental shares, manager mailboxes, an HR platform procured without IT involvement.

Former employees submit requests. Applicants who were never hired submit them too. Plaintiffs' attorneys submit them with a clear understanding that a disorganized response is itself informative. First notice frequently lands in a general inbox or the IT support queue, which means intake needs a named owner and a defined path before it is tested rather than after.

An AI assistant inherits your worst permission decision

Here is what separates 2026 from 2023. Generative assistants embedded in productivity suites rarely create new stores of personal information. They surface the ones you already have. A tool that answers questions across an organization's files and mail inherits the permission model beneath it, and in most companies that model was assembled incrementally, by people who have since left, on a quiet assumption that obscurity would do part of the work of access control.

Obscurity does none of that work now. An over-shared HR folder that was technically readable by everyone and practically invisible becomes findable in a sentence. That is the governance question at the center of Microsoft 365 deployments today, and it is a privacy question as much as a security one. Personal information that quietly becomes broadly accessible has been disclosed internally, and every additional surface it reaches is another surface a deletion request must cover.

Shadow AI compounds the problem. An employee who pastes a candidate file or a customer list into a consumer AI product has moved personal information to a processor the business never evaluated, never contracted with and cannot instruct. No processing agreement covers that transfer. Deletion workflows do not reach it. Treating the behavior as a training failure alone misreads it — the control that actually works is identity and access, which is why IT security and privacy have collapsed into a single conversation.

What to fund before the first request arrives

Four moves matter, roughly in this order, and not one of them is a software purchase.

  • Build a data map that includes staff data. Systems, categories of personal information, business purpose, retention period, named owner. Shadow SaaS counts.
  • Fix permissions before you expand AI. Broad-access groups and over-shared sites first. An assistant deployed over a loose permission model industrializes whatever exposure already exists.
  • Design retention on purpose. Data you no longer hold cannot be requested, breached or produced in discovery. Settle how deletion obligations interact with your data backup and immutability requirements on paper, with counsel, instead of improvising during a live request.
  • Rehearse one request end to end and time it. The clock produces a number, and that number describes your compliance posture more honestly than any policy document will.

Regulatory direction reinforces all four. California privacy rulemaking has been moving toward documented risk assessment, evidence of security controls rather than assertions about them, and scrutiny of automated decision-making in contexts such as hiring and performance evaluation — the categories where mid-market companies adopted AI features fastest, usually because the feature arrived inside software they already owned. Read that as a direction of travel, not a deadline, and confirm what applies to your organization, and when, with counsel.

The number that replaces your privacy policy

Privacy compliance stopped being a document in a shared folder at roughly the moment AI assistants learned to read everything inside that folder. It is now a property of how identity, access, retention and data architecture are built, which makes it a standing responsibility for whoever runs your technology rather than a project with a completion date. The executive question is no longer whether the policy is current. The question is how long your company needs to find one person across every system it owns, and whether anyone has ever measured it.
