Shadow AI Is Now an Identity Problem

Blocking AI sites did not end shadow AI. The real exposure moved to OAuth consent grants inside Microsoft 365, which makes it an identity governance issue.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
August 9, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. Blocking the domain moved the exposure rather than shrinking it
  2. A consent grant is a key, not a click
  3. The notetaker on the call is a subprocessor your contracts never named
  4. Copilot finds what was already overshared
  5. What this should change before your next leadership meeting

One screen in Microsoft Entra can tell an executive more about AI use inside the company than an employee survey will. Sign in to the admin center, open Enterprise apps, choose All applications, set the application type filter to Enterprise Applications so that Microsoft's own are left out, and read the list. Note-takers. Browser assistants. Resume screeners. Sales assistants. Applications nobody in finance ever saw an invoice for, some of them holding standing permission to read mail and files.

Executives seldom ask to see it. They ask their IT lead whether staff are using AI with company data, and receive an honest answer assembled from what people admit to. The Entra list is not assembled from what people admit to. It records what somebody approved, including the approvals nobody remembers.

That gap is the subject. Shadow AI used to mean text pasted into a chat window, and that still happens. The part that now deserves a leadership decision is delegated access, which behaves nothing like a leaked paragraph. This essay is for the executives who sign the AI policy and the IT leaders who have to make it real. The decision it informs is who may connect an outside application to company data.

Blocking the domain moved the exposure rather than shrinking it

Plenty of companies met the first AI wave the way they met consumer file sharing a decade earlier: block the domain, issue a policy, consider the matter closed. That was defensible at the time. It is much weaker now, for a structural reason.

AI is no longer only a destination employees visit. It is a feature inside software the company already bought and already trusts: the browser, the CRM, the PDF editor, the design tool, the recruiting platform, the conferencing client. When someone switches on an AI feature inside a sanctioned application, no firewall rule fires and nobody files an exception. The feature usually works with whatever access the application already holds.

Our read, offered as analysis rather than fact: a ban without a sanctioned alternative made the exposure harder to see rather than smaller. Blocked domains create an internal story that the risk was handled, and that story suppresses exactly the reporting that would show where AI entered the business. People kept using it. They stopped mentioning it.

A consent grant is a key, not a click

The mechanic deserving executive attention is the consent screen. When an employee connects a third-party AI tool to Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, they are usually not uploading anything. They are approving a request from the vendor's application for permission to read mail, files or the calendar, and sometimes to send messages on their behalf. Scopes with names like Mail.Read and Files.Read.All are not one-time favors.

Microsoft has narrowed what a single employee can approve. Under the consent policy Microsoft manages, the default for new tenants, users can no longer approve a third-party application's request to read their mail, their calendar, their chats or every file they can reach, apart from a short list of common mail apps. An administrator has to. Three gaps remain. The policy governs only tenants where that setting is selected, and an older setting still lets users approve anything that does not require an administrator. Permissions outside the list can still be granted by anyone. And a change to these settings affects only future requests, so every grant made earlier stays in force until somebody removes it.

Three consequences follow, and together they explain why this belongs to identity rather than data handling:

  • The access is continuous. The application keeps its permission after the employee's interest fades. Nobody revokes enthusiasm, and a grant one user made can be seen in the admin center but not revoked there; removing it takes Graph or PowerShell.
  • The access is impersonal. A connected application is a non-human identity operating in your tenant. The HR system does not know it exists, and neither does the onboarding checklist.
  • The access does not track your HR process. A permission one user granted works only as that user, so disabling the account cuts it off. A delegated permission an administrator approved for the whole organization keeps working for everyone else after the approver leaves. An application permission needs no signed-in user at all: with Files.Read.All granted that way, an application can read any file in the tenant. An inventory that does not separate the three is not yet an inventory of risk.

So the governance question changes shape. The job is less to police sentences than to manage a population of non-human identities that nobody onboarded and nobody reviews, the same category of risk that IT security teams already handle for service accounts and API keys. Entra ID has two settings that matter. User consent settings decide what an employee may approve alone. The admin consent workflow gives them somewhere to send everything else, as a request that named reviewers can approve, deny or block. The workflow comes into play only when a user is unable to consent, so restrict first, then switch it on. Both are configuration tasks rather than projects. The obstacle is rarely technical.

The notetaker on the call is a subprocessor your contracts never named

Meeting assistants spread quickly because they spread by invitation. One participant adds a bot to a calendar invite, and everyone else on the call is recorded and transcribed by a vendor they did not select and probably cannot name.

For much of the Los Angeles mid-market, that lands on legal ground before it lands on security ground. Entertainment and post-production work runs on confidentiality terms. Law firms protect client confidences and privilege. Healthcare organizations carry patient-privacy duties under HIPAA and state law that do not pause for a helpful transcription tool. Apparel, logistics and manufacturing companies sign customer NDAs specifying who may hold data and where it may live.

Few of those obligations were drafted with a third-party transcription vendor in mind, and none of them care that the tool was free. An uninvited recorder on a client call is, in substance, a subprocessor nobody approved. That is a defensible reading rather than a legal opinion, but it is the reading a counterparty's lawyer may reach for, and it is cheaper to write your own rule than to answer theirs. California's consent rule and the retention side of the problem are covered in Who Can Search Your Meeting Transcripts.

Copilot finds what was already overshared

The sanctioned path carries its own trap, and it catches companies that did everything else right.

Microsoft is explicit that Copilot, formerly Microsoft 365 Copilot, surfaces only organizational data the user already has permission to view. The difficulty is that in a tenant with years of accumulated sharing, what people technically have the right to open can be far wider than anyone intended. The team site that was set to public. The project library inherited two reorganizations ago. The HR folder opened up once for convenience and never closed.

Nobody found those files before, because finding them required knowing they existed. A capable retrieval engine removes that friction. The compensation spreadsheet, the acquisition model, the disciplinary record: each becomes one plain-language question away from someone who was never meant to see it.

Permissions hygiene is therefore a precondition for AI adoption rather than a follow-up task. That means site access reviews, oversharing reports, restricted sites, sensitivity labels on what matters, and a genuine look at who inherited access to what. It is unglamorous work. It is also the difference between an assistant that compounds your advantage and an incident you explain to your board.

What this should change before your next leadership meeting

The strategic error is treating shadow AI as a discipline problem. In our reading, most employees using it are not being reckless. They found tools that made their work faster, and their employer offered nothing better. Prediction, labeled as such: enforcement-first approaches will keep losing, because shadow AI lives in the gap between the tool people are allowed to use and the tool that is available to them, and only one side of that gap is under your control.

  1. Read the list. Open it yourself, with whoever runs the tenant, rather than hearing it summarized. Sort each application into user-granted, admin-approved or application permission.
  2. Restrict consent, then open a door. Limit what users can approve, turn on the admin consent workflow, name the reviewers, and publish how quickly a request gets an answer.
  3. Clear the backlog. Remove grants nobody claims. A revoked permission can be granted again while user consent stays open, which is why this is the third step.
  4. Set a rule on recording bots. Decide which client meetings an outside assistant may join before a client sets the rule for you.
  5. Remediate oversharing before switching on Copilot. Then give people a fast, approved, well-supported AI path.

Judge the result by two things: every application on the list has an owner and a reason, and a consent request is answered while the employee still wants the tool. A good tool with governance beats a policy everyone routes around.
