Your AI Vendor Holds a Key to Your Data

Vendor questionnaires describe the vendor's controls. Your exposure is the permission an AI application holds in your own tenant, and who is allowed to grant it.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
August 31, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
8 min read
In this article
  1. The questionnaire audits the vendor's building, not your keys
  2. Ask what the token can do on a bad day
  3. Autonomy is a security property, not a pricing tier
  4. Machine identities do not resign
  5. Where AI vendor risk should actually live

A director of operations signs up for an AI meeting assistant on a Tuesday afternoon. The consent screen asks to read her calendar, join meetings on her behalf, and reach the files she can reach. Her company still lets users approve applications themselves, so she clicks Accept, because the alternative is failing to evaluate the tool she was asked to evaluate. Nothing in that moment triggers a vendor review, a contract, or a conversation with IT.

Three floors away, procurement is in week three of a security questionnaire long enough to need its own project plan, for a different AI product that will read exactly one exported spreadsheet.

Both are real controls. Only one is pointed at the actual exposure. That mismatch is the shape of AI vendor risk in 2026, and it is not a story about careless vendors. Due diligence built for software that stores your data is being applied to software that acts with your identity. This essay is for the executives and procurement leads who approve AI tools. The decision it informs is what to ask before signature, and which part of the company should own the answer.

The questionnaire audits the vendor's building, not your keys

A SOC 2 report covers controls at a service organization in areas such as security, availability and confidentiality, and an ISO 27001 certificate says the vendor's management of information security was audited against that standard. Both are useful, and a vendor who can produce neither deserves scrutiny. Read them carefully, though. They are facts about the vendor. They are not facts about you.

The traditional question (where does our data live, who can see it, how is it encrypted) assumes a one-directional relationship. You send data out, the vendor holds it, and your exposure equals the size of the pile you sent.

Many AI tools inverted that. The application still copies what it processes and may keep some of it, but the copy is no longer the whole exposure. It also wants standing, delegated authority to reach into your environment and retrieve what it needs, whenever it needs it, under the identity of the person who approved it. Whoever holds that key can refill the pile at any time.

This is analysis rather than a measured finding, and it is easy to test inside your own organization. Pull the last three AI security reviews your company completed and look for two artifacts: the list of permission scopes the application requested, and a log showing what it has done since. In Microsoft 365 the standard audit log holds some of that. Microsoft Graph activity logs record every request made through Graph, but only if someone turned them on. Each entry shows the application's identity and, where there is one, the user's, and collecting them takes an Entra ID P1 or P2 license and somewhere to send the data. If neither artifact is in the packet, the review examined someone else's infrastructure and called it your risk assessment.

Ask what the token can do on a bad day

Reframe the first question. "Is this vendor secure" is one nobody can answer honestly. Ask this instead: assume the vendor is breached on a Thursday, and say what the attacker inherits.

That has a precise answer, and it is written in the permission scopes granted to the application. In a Microsoft 365 environment, an app holding delegated permissions acts only as a signed-in user, within what that user can reach. An app holding application permissions acts with no user present and can reach everything the permission covers. The two are different orders of risk: a contained incident versus reach across the entire corpus. Both look identical on a vendor's trust page, and neither appears on a pricing sheet.

A vendor that can operate under narrowed permissions, and helps you narrow them, is telling you something real about how the product was built. When the answer is "full access makes the experience better," that is also telling you something real.

Then follow the chain. Many AI products are applications sitting on foundation models licensed from someone else, sometimes several someone elses depending on the task. The tier matters as much as the name. OpenAI says it does not train on business data by default and may hold API inputs and outputs for up to 30 days; its terms for individual accounts differ. Ask which tier the vendor buys, what the retention window is at each hop, and whether each promise is a contractual commitment or a setting someone can toggle.

Ask, too, what happens when the vendor changes models, because a swap can move your data to a new processor with no amendment to your agreement. Microsoft's own Copilot shows how ordinary this has become. Anthropic's Claude models now run inside it with Anthropic as a Microsoft subprocessor, on by default for most commercial customers outside the EU, EFTA and UK, excluded from the EU Data Boundary, and removable by an administrator. A separate class of models that retains data stays off until an administrator accepts Anthropic's own terms. None of that is improper, and all of it is documented. It is simply something a questionnaire answered last year could not have told you. In our judgment, a change-of-subprocessor notice clause is worth more in an AI contract than most of the encryption language above it.

Autonomy is a security property, not a pricing tier

The category shift of the last two years is that AI tools moved from summarizing to doing: sending the email, updating the record, routing the invoice, moving the file. Vendors market this as capability. Treat it as a control question.

Three properties determine whether an acting agent is governable. Reversibility: can an action be undone, and does the product tell you which actions cannot be. Approval gates: can a human be required in the loop for a defined class of actions, configured by you rather than by vendor default. Attribution: when the agent acts, what does the log say.

Attribution is the sleeper. If an agent operates under a human's delegated identity, your audit trail may show a user performing actions the user never performed. During an investigation, that destroys the timeline you were going to use to reconstruct what happened. Any IT security program built around identity as the perimeter should require that agent actions are distinguishable from human actions in the log before the tool touches production.

Machine identities do not resign

Run the offboarding before you sign. When the contract ends, what happens to the service principal, the API keys, the refresh tokens, and the derived data, meaning the embeddings and indexes built from your documents that are no longer your documents in any recognizable form? Deletion of source content and deletion of everything computed from it are separate commitments, and a contract may cover only the first.

Consented applications persist quietly in a directory long after the person who approved them has moved on, still holding whatever they were granted. Nothing prompts a review. The grant has no expiry and no exit interview. A quarterly walk of the consent register (every application holding a grant, what that grant reaches, who approved it, when it was last examined) is, in our judgment, among the highest-return hours available to a mid-sized company. How to read that list is covered in Shadow AI Is Now an Identity Problem.

Where AI vendor risk should actually live

The executive decision here is where this category of risk gets governed. Housed in procurement, you will get thorough answers about someone else's data center while consent grants accumulate in your tenant unreviewed. Housed in identity governance, the picture inverts: you hold a register of every application with a key, what that key opens, who granted it, and when anyone last looked.

Two changes make that real, and neither is a large project. Restrict who can consent, so that granting broad access becomes a decision by a small named group rather than a click by anyone. Require that any AI tool with write or send authority logs agent actions distinguishably from human ones. Ownership is the harder part. Self-service adoption selects for precisely the tools that skip review, which is arithmetic rather than a criticism of anyone's diligence. The first visible symptom often arrives as a user question to the help desk: why is this app asking for an administrator to approve it?

Judge the change after a quarter. Every AI tool in use should appear in the register with an owner, a scope and a review date, and the next questionnaire should ask about your tenant before it asks about the vendor's building.
