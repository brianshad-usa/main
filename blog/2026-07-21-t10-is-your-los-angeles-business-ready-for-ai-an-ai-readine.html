Is Your LA Business Ready for AI? Checklist

AI readiness is mostly about who can reach which data, how people sign in and who owns the result, not about faster hardware.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
July 21, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
4 min read
In this article
  1. Check who can reach which data
  2. Check identity and security basics
  3. Write the rules people will follow
  4. Pick a problem worth solving
  5. Be realistic about infrastructure and cost
  6. Readiness at a glance
  7. How to judge the pilot

Being ready for AI has little to do with buying faster hardware. For most Los Angeles businesses using hosted AI tools, readiness comes down to five things: who can reach which data, how people sign in, what the rules are, which problem the tool is meant to solve, and who is accountable for the result. This checklist is for owners and executives deciding whether to roll out an AI assistant and what to fix first. It is a self-review, not an audit, and the order of the items reflects analysis of where problems usually appear.

Check who can reach which data

This is the item most often skipped and the one with the largest consequence. AI assistants that work with company content generally respect the permissions already in place, which means they inherit your sharing mistakes. Microsoft states that Copilot only surfaces organizational data to which individual users have at least view permissions. A shared folder that "everyone" can open, an old project site with salary spreadsheets, or a former contractor with lingering access now becomes searchable in plain English. Microsoft's own deployment guidance for Copilot names remediating oversharing as its first pillar.

Before rollout, review sharing links, broad groups such as "everyone", guest accounts, and sites that hold HR, finance or legal material. Fix those first, then decide who gets access to the AI tool.

Check identity and security basics

An AI assistant is another way into your data for anyone who can sign in as a user. Confirm that multi-factor authentication is required for all accounts, that former employees are disabled promptly, that administrator accounts are few and protected, and that you know which devices are used to reach company data. Review cybersecurity basics before adding new tools; a short review of identity, email protection and sharing settings usually reveals what to fix. If the assistant is a third-party product, ask the vendor where data is processed, how long it is kept, whether it is used to train models, and what logs you can see.

Write the rules people will follow

A one-page acceptable-use policy covers most of what a small organization needs: which tools are approved, what data must never be entered, which outputs require human review, and whom to ask. If you handle regulated information such as patient records (see healthcare IT services for the technical side), involve your attorney or compliance adviser before staff use any AI tool with it; the regulations that apply, and the agreements a vendor must sign, depend on your circumstances. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes a voluntary AI Risk Management Framework that larger organizations use to structure these decisions.

Pick a problem worth solving

Vague goals such as "use AI" produce expensive experiments. State one process, the current cost in hours or errors, and the improvement you would count as success. Good first candidates have a bounded task, content you already own and a person who can check the answer. Poor ones depend on missing or inconsistent data. Data quality matters here, but you do not need a data warehouse: the practical question is whether the documents and records the tool will draw on are current, findable and owned by someone.

Be realistic about infrastructure and cost

Many readiness guides say you need powerful servers and large storage. For hosted assistants that is usually not true, because the heavy processing happens in the vendor's cloud. Your constraints are more likely licenses, a reliable internet connection at each office, compatible cloud services, and support staff who can answer questions. Budget for subscriptions, training time, review effort and ongoing administration, and set a date to decide whether to renew. Include the tool in your backup and recovery thinking if it creates or stores business records, and make sure your IT support team knows who owns it.

Readiness at a glance

What ready looks like, and what to fix first
AreaReady looks likeFix first if
Data accessSharing reviewed; sensitive sites restricted to named groupsCompany-wide links or "everyone" access on sensitive content
IdentityMFA on all accounts; leavers disabled the same dayShared logins or accounts of former staff still active
PolicyWritten rules on approved tools, prohibited data and reviewStaff already use personal AI accounts with company data
Use caseOne process with a measured baseline and a named ownerThe goal is "try AI" with no measure of success
Vendor termsWritten answers on data use, retention and loggingThe terms were never read
SupportSomeone owns training, questions and renewal decisionsThe pilot depends on one enthusiastic employee

How to judge the pilot

Run a pilot with a small group for about two months. Record time saved per task, how often a person had to correct the output, data-handling incidents, and user feedback. Decide in advance what result justifies expanding, and who has authority to stop. If the pilot succeeds only because one person spends hours checking its work, the saving is smaller than it looks. Consider the managed IT services or internal IT team's findings from the data-access review as inputs to the decision, not as an afterthought.
