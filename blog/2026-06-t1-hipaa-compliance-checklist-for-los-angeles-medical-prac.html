HIPAA Compliance Checklist for Los Angeles Medical Practices in 2026

A neutral walk through the Security Rule's required and addressable items, written as questions a practice can check before speaking to counsel.

This HIPAA checklist for Los Angeles medical practices summarizes what the federal Security Rule asks of a practice that handles electronic protected health information (ePHI), and turns each point into a question you can check. It is for practice administrators, physicians and office managers who want to see where they stand before speaking to counsel or an assessor. It is general information, not legal advice. No technology provider can make a practice compliant on its own. Your attorney or a qualified HIPAA assessor decides what the rules require of your practice.

What the rule requires and where it allows judgement

The Security Rule groups its requirements into administrative, physical and technical safeguards. Some specifications are "required". Others are "addressable", which means the practice must implement them or document why an alternative is reasonable. In the regulation text, for example, unique user identification is required, while automatic logoff and encryption are addressable. Many checklists present every item as mandatory; the accurate position is that the rule is flexible but expects a documented decision. A proposed update to the Security Rule has been in the federal rulemaking process and could tighten several of these points, so ask counsel about its current status.

Administrative safeguards

These are the policies, assignments and routines that govern how your staff handle ePHI. The rule requires a risk analysis, described as an accurate and thorough assessment of potential risks and vulnerabilities, a designated security official, and a security awareness and training program for all workforce members, including management.

  • A named security official, and a named privacy official, who may be the same person in a small practice
  • A written risk analysis that lists where ePHI is created, stored and sent, the threats and vulnerabilities, and the actions taken. The rule does not fix a frequency; a common practice is to repeat it when systems change and on a regular schedule your adviser recommends
  • Training for every workforce member at hire and on a schedule, with attendance records
  • Role-based access, so each person can open only the records their job needs, and a documented process to remove access when someone leaves
  • Written policies for the topics above, reviewed when practices change

Physical safeguards

The physical safeguard standards cover facility access, workstation use and security, and device and media controls. In a busy clinic, the practical questions are who can walk past a screen and what happens to equipment at end of life.

  • Controlled access to server closets, records rooms and areas where ePHI is displayed
  • Screens positioned or filtered so patients and visitors cannot read them, and workstations that lock when unattended
  • An inventory of laptops, tablets, phones and removable media that hold ePHI
  • A documented process for wiping or destroying drives and media before disposal or reuse
  • Locked storage and secure shredding for paper records

Technical safeguards

These are the controls built into systems. The regulation names access control, audit controls, integrity and transmission security. Encryption and automatic logoff are addressable specifications, so document your decision for each. Multi-factor authentication is not named in the current rule, but it is widely used to meet the access-control and risk-management expectations, and it is sensible for any system that holds patient data.

  • A unique login for each person, and no shared accounts on systems with ePHI
  • Multi-factor authentication on email, the records system and remote access
  • Automatic logoff after inactivity, or a documented reason for a different arrangement
  • Encryption of laptops, phones and backups, and of messages that carry ePHI, or a documented alternative
  • Audit logs that record who opened which records, with a person assigned to review them
  • Prompt updates to operating systems and applications
  • Backups that are tested by restoring them, with at least one copy kept away from the main system

Vendors and business associates

A vendor that creates, receives, maintains or transmits ePHI for your practice is a business associate, and the rule requires satisfactory assurances, in practice a written business associate agreement. That includes billing companies, transcription services, cloud storage and the provider that administers your systems. Microsoft's documentation notes that there is no HHS-approved certification showing that a business associate complies with HIPAA, so a vendor's marketing claim is not evidence; read the agreement and the list of services it covers. The same Microsoft page says using its services does not on its own achieve compliance for the customer.

  • A signed business associate agreement for every vendor that touches ePHI, with a list kept current
  • Confirmation that the specific service you use is covered, not only the vendor in general
  • A review of what each vendor does if it has an incident, and how fast it must tell you

Incidents and breach notification

Write an incident-response plan before you need one: who decides whether an event is a reportable breach, who contacts patients, and who engages counsel. Under the Breach Notification Rule, a covered entity must notify affected individuals without unreasonable delay and in no case later than 60 calendar days after discovery. The 60 days is an outer limit, not a target. Notification to the Department of Health and Human Services and, for larger breaches, the media has its own rules; your attorney should advise, and California law adds obligations of its own. Pro Link Systems publishes an incident-response playbook free and ungated that you can adapt as a starting template.

Documentation

The rule requires that you retain required documentation for six years from creation or the date it was last in effect, whichever is later. Keep the risk analysis, policies, training records, signed business associate agreements, incident reports and decisions on addressable items together where an authorized person can find them.

Where this checklist stops

A checklist shows gaps; it does not decide what your practice is obliged to do. Bring the results to your attorney or a qualified assessor, who can judge your obligations, including California law and any contracts with insurers or health systems. A managed IT services provider can help you implement technical controls, and healthcare IT services describes that area, but the legal determination stays with qualified counsel. Your IT support team can run the access, backup and logging checks above and report the results.
