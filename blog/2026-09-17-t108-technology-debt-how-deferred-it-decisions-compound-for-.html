Technology Debt Has a New Interest Rate

Technology debt stopped being an equipment problem. In 2026 it compounds in identity, permissions and data, and an AI pilot is what finally exposes it.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 17, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The debt stopped living in the server room
  2. Deferred purchases depreciate. Deferred decisions compound.
  3. An AI pilot is the audit nobody commissioned
  4. What Los Angeles growth patterns leave behind
  5. Where the interest actually shows up

Key takeaways

  • Today’s technology debt lives in configuration (identity, permissions, data placement), and nothing sends a notice when it ages.
  • Deferred decisions compound: each one constrains the next, and the interest is paid in decision latency.
  • An AI pilot works as an unplanned audit, surfacing permission sprawl in front of its executive sponsor.

When a three-week AI pilot takes four months, look upstream of the AI.

The sequence is familiar to anyone who has run one. Before an assistant can be pointed at a document library, somebody has to establish which sites hold salary data. Then comes the question of why a shared mailbox left over from an old reorganization still reaches the executive drive. After that, whether the dozen service accounts nobody can account for are safe to disable. None of that is artificial intelligence work. All of it is the bill for decisions that were reasonable to postpone at the time.

That is what technology debt has become: less a matter of aging hardware than of accumulated, undocumented choices, now being called in by a class of technology that did not exist when they were made. For the executive deciding what to fund next, the useful question is which of those choices is already charging interest.

The debt stopped living in the server room

Technology debt used to be visible. A server aged. Phone systems went end-of-life. Vendors announced sunset dates, and finance could put a number against the replacement. That kind still exists, and it still needs a replacement schedule. Debt with a maturity date is manageable debt.

Much of the rest has moved into infrastructure someone else maintains and patches. The current generation of debt lives in configuration: identity, permissions, data placement, and the quiet exceptions made to keep somebody working on a Friday afternoon. Configuration does not age out, and nothing sends a notice. A Microsoft 365 tenant stood up years ago and extended continuously since then still runs, which is precisely the problem. Systems that work generate no pressure to examine them.

This is analysis rather than measurement: the more capable the platform, the longer a bad configuration can survive undetected inside it. Cloud platforms absorb a remarkable amount of accumulated mess before anything visibly breaks.

Deferred purchases depreciate. Deferred decisions compound.

Executives tend to model technology debt as deferred capital expenditure. Postpone the refresh, carry higher maintenance for a while, replace the asset later at a somewhat worse price. Linear, forecastable, and something a CFO can reason about on a single page.

Decisions behave differently. Each deferral constrains the next. Skip a deliberate identity design and the file structure gets built around the workarounds. That structure then dictates how the next application is integrated. Integration in turn sets the ceiling on what your cybersecurity controls can enforce, because Conditional Access cannot be applied cleanly where nobody can say who should have access to what.

Interest on this debt is paid first in decision latency: the widening gap between choosing to do something and being able to do it. That cost appears on no financial statement, right up to the day a competitor moves faster on the same opportunity with the same budget.

The cheapest deferral available in a given quarter is often the one that destroys the most future optionality. Legacy authentication is the clearest example. Keeping it alive for one stubborn line-of-business application costs nothing this month, but the protocols involved do not support multifactor authentication, so the exception holds a door open on the identity perimeter for as long as it stays there. Some of these loans get called by the lender. Microsoft has disabled Basic authentication in every Exchange Online tenant for most protocols, and has announced plans to retire the main survivor, Basic authentication for sending mail over SMTP. A scanner or billing system still sending with a stored password carries a due date someone else sets.

An AI pilot is the audit nobody commissioned

Microsoft Copilot surfaces only organizational data that the individual user already has permission to view. That is the right architecture. What it means in practice is that the assistant inherits access without inheriting judgment, restraint, or institutional memory about which folder is off-limits. Agents widen the question, because some use delegated access on behalf of a signed-in user and others act under an identity of their own. Ask which, before approving one.

An employee with excessive access may never exercise it. Habit, more than discipline, keeps people inside the few locations they actually use. Ask an assistant a broad question and it draws on whatever that identity is permitted to see, then summarizes the result helpfully. Permission sprawl that sat harmless for years becomes a retrieval surface the moment something is built to traverse it at speed.

So AI deployment functions as an audit nobody commissioned. Where identity hygiene has been deliberate, a pilot has less to trip over. Where a decade of exceptions has accumulated, the debt surfaces mid-pilot, in front of an executive sponsor who was promised a productivity story and handed a governance project instead.

Opinion, stated as such: this is the most useful thing AI has done for mid-market IT so far. Abstract risk gets deferred indefinitely. A stalled initiative with a sponsor's name on it gets funded.

What Los Angeles growth patterns leave behind

The composition of this debt is not random. In a 20-to-500 seat Los Angeles business it tends to follow recognizable growth patterns. The four below are archetypes drawn from how companies in this market grow, not descriptions of any particular organization.

  • Acquisitions that were never actually merged. Two tenants, two identity providers, one payroll system, and a trust relationship configured as a temporary measure several years ago.
  • Distributed footprints. A San Fernando Valley headquarters, a second site across the basin, project staff somewhere else again, and access rules written for one office and patched for the rest.
  • Third-party access as a business requirement. Entertainment, legal and healthcare workflows all depend on outside collaborators. Each engagement can leave behind a guest or machine identity that outlives the work it was created for.
  • Capital that went to the lease. Where occupancy cost dominates the budget, infrastructure is an easy line to defer. Defensible for years, and rarely revisited on purpose.

Every item on that list was a correct call at the time, made by competent people under real constraints. Technology debt is rarely a monument to negligence. Mostly it is the residue of good decisions that were never scheduled for review.

Where the interest actually shows up

The decision this should inform is not whether to remediate everything. Nobody funds that, and nobody should. What matters is knowing what you are carrying, and at what rate. A short register, one line per debt, is enough to start.

Configuration debt: where it sits, how it surfaces, what to decide
DebtWhere it sitsWhat surfaces itThe decision to schedule
Standing accessGroups, site permissions, guest accountsAn assistant that summarizes a file its reader was never meant to findWho owns each sensitive site, and when its access is next reviewed
Legacy authenticationOlder applications, scanners, scriptsA policy that cannot be enforced, or a vendor's retirement dateReplace, isolate or retire, with a date
Non-human identitiesService accounts, app registrations, API keysAn integration nobody dares to disableA named owner for each
ExceptionsPolicy exclusions, temporary trusts, admin rightsAn insurer's form, an audit question, or an incidentAn expiry date, set when the exception is granted
Data placementMigrated shares, unowned sites, archivesA retrieval result or a discovery requestKeep, move or delete, by class of data

Three questions, in our judgment, then produce most of the clarity an executive needs. Who holds standing access to financial and personnel data, and when was that list last reviewed by a person rather than inherited by default? Which applications cannot support modern authentication, and what is each one costing in enforcement you are unable to apply? If your data backup restored cleanly tomorrow, would it restore the permission structure you want, or the one you have?

A business that can answer those three is carrying manageable debt. One that cannot is not carrying less. It is carrying debt at an interest rate nobody has measured.

The structural change worth stating, offered as analysis: technology debt used to announce itself when something broke. Increasingly it announces itself when something new is attempted. The second failure mode is harder to plan around, and more expensive to discover in the middle of a board-sponsored initiative.

Companies that handle this well, whatever the age of their systems, treat configuration as a set of decisions with named owners and review dates, supported by IT support that documents exceptions rather than quietly accumulating them. The practical first step is small: run one access review on the data your next initiative will touch, before the sponsor sees the first demonstration.
