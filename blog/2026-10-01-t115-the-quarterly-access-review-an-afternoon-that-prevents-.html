Access Reviews Should Default to Revoke

The quarterly access review audits employees while the tenant fills with apps, service accounts and agents.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
October 1, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The identities nobody counted
  2. Access accumulates and almost nothing removes it
  3. Flip the default from keep to revoke
  4. The signature belongs to the business, not to IT
  5. What four afternoons a year actually buy

Ask your IT lead who still has access to the shared finance folder, and you will have an answer by the end of the day. Put the same question about which connected applications hold standing permission to read every mailbox in the tenant, and the answer may take a week, if it arrives at all.

That asymmetry is the problem with the quarterly access review as many companies still run it. The exercise was designed for a world where access meant a person and a folder. Today it runs against an environment where a growing share of the things holding permissions are not people at all, and where the people who do hold permissions were granted many of them for jobs they no longer perform.

A longer checklist will not fix that. Three decisions will, and they belong to the executive who sponsors the review: what it covers, what happens when nobody answers, and whose signature counts.

The identities nobody counted

Walk through what accumulates in a hypothetical 150-seat environment. User accounts, obviously. Underneath them sits everything that was never on a staff list:

  • Service accounts created for a line-of-business integration years ago, with credentials nobody rotates because nobody is certain what breaks.
  • OAuth app consents (the project management tool, the e-signature platform, the scheduling add-in, the note-taking assistant someone wired into a calendar), each carrying a permission scope granted once and not revisited.
  • API keys issued to a developer who has since moved to a different team.
  • Automation flows running under the credentials of whoever built them.

Not one of these has a last day. Offboarding, the lifecycle event most likely to remove access, does not apply to them.

Agents belong on that list as well, and it matters which kind. An assistant that uses delegated permissions acts for a signed-in user and cannot reach anything that user could not. An agent with application permissions acts as itself, with no user present, and can reach any data the permission covers. The delegated case deserves more thought than it gets. This is analysis, but it follows directly from the model: an over-permissioned user is a risk bounded by one person's judgment, attention, and working hours. That same user wired to an agent becomes an over-permissioned process, one that can traverse everything the identity can reach, quickly and repeatedly, without the social friction that stops most people from opening a folder they know is not theirs.

Deploy Copilot on a Microsoft 365 tenant whose permissions have never been pruned, and you have not created a new exposure. You have made an old one far easier to exercise. Deploy an agent with an identity of its own and you have added an account, which belongs on the inventory from its first day.

Access accumulates and almost nothing removes it

Consider how permission actually grows. Someone joins the finance team and receives the finance group. A cover assignment during a colleague's leave adds payroll. A move into operations adds the operations group, and nobody removes finance, because removal requires a person to notice and a person to decide, and neither is anyone's job. A vendor relationship adds a shared mailbox. Four years in, that employee holds the access footprint of three roles they no longer perform.

Multiply that by every tenured employee and the picture is clear enough without a statistic. Departing staff get disabled; staying staff tend to keep everything they ever touched.

That accumulated reach shapes the cost of your next incident. Credential compromise and business email compromise do not care about headcount. They care what one compromised identity can touch. The distance between a contained event and a disclosure-triggering one can be nothing more than whether the account the attacker landed on still had reach into something it stopped needing years earlier. Permission pruning is blast-radius management. It is also inexpensive, which may be why it loses to work that arrives with a purchase order attached.

Flip the default from keep to revoke

Here is the change that makes the quarterly review worth the afternoon. Many reviews run on an implicit default: access is retained unless someone objects. A list goes out, nobody has time to study it, nobody objects, everything stays. The exercise produces an artifact and changes nothing.

Invert that. Access is revoked unless a named person affirmatively justifies keeping it. The burden of proof moves from removal to retention. In Microsoft Entra the choice is a literal setting: each access review specifies what happens if reviewers do not respond, with options for no change, remove access, approve access, or take the system's recommendation. Microsoft attaches a warning to the revoking options, because combined with automatic application they can remove everyone's access to a resource whose reviewers stay silent. Start with low-risk groups for that reason.

Executives resist the inversion because it sounds disruptive. In our judgment the disruption is small and self-correcting, because access nobody will defend is usually access nobody is using. When a revocation does break something, it surfaces as a help desk ticket and gets restored with a documented business reason attached. That ticket is not a failure of the process. It converts an undocumented standing permission into a justified one, on the record. Two exceptions need naming in advance. Emergency access accounts are reviewed by hand and never removed by silence. And some access is used once a year, at year-end close, open enrollment or audit, so it looks dormant in every other quarter.

Two structural moves shrink how much there is to review at all: privileged access granted just in time rather than held standing, and Conditional Access policies that gate sensitive resources on device and risk signals, so a valid credential alone is not sufficient. Neither replaces the review, because neither answers whether a given person should hold a given entitlement. Microsoft's tooling covers group membership, application assignments, directory and Azure roles, and access packages, and it requires Microsoft Entra ID Governance or Entra Suite licensing, with some capabilities available under Entra ID P2. Third-party consents and keys issued inside other products are not on that list. Plan a separate line for them.

The signature belongs to the business, not to IT

The most common way a well-intentioned review fails, in our view, is that IT runs it alone. An administrator can tell you with precision that a controller holds read-write access to a particular SharePoint site. No administrator can tell you whether she should. That judgment lives with whoever owns the data.

Route the review by data owner rather than by system. Finance attests to finance systems, HR to personnel records, the clinical or legal or operational lead to their own. IT security supplies the inventory, flags the anomalies, and executes the decisions; the business makes them. Then keep the artifact: a dated record of who reviewed what, what was revoked, and what was justified. Pull your own cyber insurance application and read the access-control questions on it; they are easier to answer honestly when that record exists.

How often to do it depends on who is asking. Outside references differ in how specific they are, and healthcare organizations, financial institutions and their counsel should confirm which apply.

What outside references say about reviewing access
ReferenceWhat it asks forStated cadence
Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls v8.1, 5.1 and 5.5An account inventory covering users, administrators and service accounts; validate that active accounts are authorizedAt least quarterly
CIS Controls v8.1, 6.8Role-based access reviewed so that all privileges are authorizedAt least annually
Federal Trade Commission Safeguards RuleImplement and periodically review access controlsPeriodic; no interval given
HIPAA Security Rule, 45 CFR 164.308(a)(4) (addressable)Establish, document, review and modify a user's right of accessNo interval given

A review that was never documented is very hard to prove. That is an observation about how evidence works, not legal advice.

What four afternoons a year actually buy

This work is unglamorous. It generates no dashboard, closes no deal, and rarely appears in a board deck as a win. What it buys is the difference between an incident a response team contains and one that becomes a notification event with counsel on the call. The whole program fits on a single page:

  1. Inventory. People, guests, administrators, service accounts, application consents, keys and agents, each with a business owner.
  2. Exempt by name. Emergency accounts and once-a-year access, each with a reason and the date it is next needed.
  3. Route. Send each data owner only their own systems. IT supplies the list and does not decide.
  4. Remove by default. No justification, no access. Begin with one low-risk group, with restore instructions ready at the help desk.
  5. Record. Date, reviewer, what was removed, what was kept and why.

Four afternoons a year, and the second should go faster than the first. Measure it by two numbers: how much was removed, and how little had to come back.

An opinion, offered as such: when a breach goes badly, the question worth asking afterward is seldom which product was missing. It is what one compromised account was still allowed to reach, and who had last been asked to defend that.
