IT Support for El Segundo Aerospace Firms

Contract clauses, not technology, set the IT scope for El Segundo defense suppliers. What DFARS, CMMC and ITAR ask, and what to require from an IT provider.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
August 3, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
4 min read
In this article
  1. The rules that set the scope
  2. Where controlled data may live in Microsoft 365
  3. Questions for an IT provider
  4. Keep operations running during the work

IT support for El Segundo aerospace and defense contractors is shaped by contract clauses before it is shaped by technology: what your contracts say about controlled information decides which systems are in scope, who may administer them and what you must report. This article is for contracts managers, security officers and operations executives at El Segundo suppliers, many of which are subcontractors to larger primes. It explains the main rules in plain terms and the decisions they force on IT. It is not legal advice and it does not certify anything; confirm which requirements apply with your contracting officer, prime contractor and qualified counsel or assessors.

The rules that set the scope

Three regimes come up most often, and they overlap.

  • DFARS clause 252.204-7012. Where it applies, the clause requires covered systems to meet NIST SP 800-171, requires cyber incidents to be reported to the Department of Defense within 72 hours of discovery, requires images of affected systems to be preserved for at least 90 days, and requires an external cloud provider that handles covered defense information to meet security requirements equivalent to the FedRAMP Moderate baseline.
  • CMMC (clause 252.204-7021). This clause requires the contractor to hold and maintain a current CMMC status at the level the contracting officer specifies, to record an annual affirmation of continuous compliance, and to flow the right level down to subcontractors that handle federal contract information or controlled unclassified information (CUI). The phase-in of which level is required in which contracts has been scheduled in stages and adjusted; read the clause in your contract and ask your contracting officer or counsel which level and date apply to you.
  • ITAR. The International Traffic in Arms Regulations control defense technical data and who may access it, including foreign-person access by administrators, support staff and cloud operators. Counsel with export-control experience should decide what data is covered.

NIST SP 800-171, the security standard these clauses draw on, is titled Protecting Controlled Unclassified Information in Nonfederal Systems and Organizations. Revision 2 has been superseded by Revision 3, so check which revision your contract names. Classified information is governed by separate government programs and is outside this article.

Where controlled data may live in Microsoft 365

The first technical decision is the cloud environment. Microsoft's own description is the safest starting point, because it states what each environment is designed for.

Microsoft cloud environments, as Microsoft describes them
EnvironmentMicrosoft's descriptionConsider when
Microsoft 365 commercialSupports CMMC Level 1 requirementsOnly federal contract information, with no CUI
Microsoft 365 GCCMeets FedRAMP High and DFARS cloud requirements; not suitable for CUI Specified such as ITAR dataCUI that is not export-controlled, after counsel confirms
Microsoft 365 GCC HighSupports CMMC Level 2 and 3 requirements when configured appropriately; supports ITARExport-controlled or other CUI Specified data

Microsoft states that CMMC compliance depends on customer configuration and operational controls, and that it does not certify partners' offerings, so the environment alone settles nothing. The trade-offs are cost, reduced feature parity with commercial services, and migration effort (Microsoft suggests allowing at least three months). A smaller enclave holding only the controlled data can reduce cost, but Microsoft cautions that most spillage happens through email and personal storage outside the enclave. Decide what data enters the boundary, and write down how people are prevented from moving it out.

Questions for an IT provider

An outside provider that touches your boundary becomes part of your scope. Ask these before signing:

  • Will any person or tool with administrator access to our controlled environment be outside the group counsel has approved, for example foreign persons or offshore support?
  • Where are remote-management tools, logs and backups of the controlled environment stored, and by whom?
  • Who writes and maintains the system security plan and plan of action, and who signs the annual affirmation? (Under the CMMC clause an affirming official in your company does.)
  • If a cyber incident occurs, how will you notify us, and can we meet the 72-hour reporting window and the 90-day image preservation requirement?
  • Do you have evidence of experience with your assessor or prime's requirements, and will you work with the assessor we choose?
  • Are backups of the controlled environment kept in the same boundary, and have you shown us a restore?

A security clearance matters only for classified work. For CUI and export-controlled data the relevant question is who is permitted to see it, which is a US-person and need-to-know decision, not a clearance one.

Keep operations running during the work

Defense programs often run to fixed delivery dates, so recovery targets belong in the plan. Agree with program managers which systems must return first, how long each may be down, and rehearse the restore. Keep cybersecurity measures that are easy to evidence: multi-factor authentication, network segmentation between engineering and the general office, and patch and log records that an assessor could read.
