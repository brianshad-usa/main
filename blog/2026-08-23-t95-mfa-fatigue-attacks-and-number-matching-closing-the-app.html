Where MFA Fatigue Attacks Went Next

Number matching ended blind push spam. Attackers moved to proxy phishing, device codes, app consents and enrollment resets, where the prompt is not the control.

Pro Link SystemsInsights

7 min read
In this article

  2. The authentication paths that number matching never touches
  3. Enrollment is now your highest-privilege operation

Number matching worked. Security programs rarely absorb that part, because a control that succeeds quietly never gets a retrospective.

The attack it defeated, known as push bombing or multi-factor authentication (MFA) fatigue, was a cheap one. An attacker holding a password from a credential dump fired approval prompts at a phone until the owner tapped Approve to make the buzzing stop. No malware. No exploit. Just attrition against a tired person at eleven at night. Then the approval prompt started demanding a number shown on the screen that initiated the sign-in, and reflexive approval stopped being possible. Microsoft now applies number matching to every Authenticator push notification, with no opt-out for users.

So the money moved. That is the most dependable law in this field, and it is the actual subject here. For the IT leader who owns MFA and the executive who funds it, the question is which of the remaining paths to close first.

What number matching proves, and what it does not

The control does one thing well. It requires whoever approves a sign-in to know the number displayed where the sign-in started. A user can no longer consent by reflex to an authentication they know nothing about.

Notice the boundary of that guarantee. Number matching proves knowledge of a number. It does not prove destination, and it does not prove who supplied the number. If the sign-in page the user is reading happens to be a proxy operated by an attacker, the number on screen is the real number, relayed from the real identity provider a fraction of a second earlier. The victim types it correctly. Authentication succeeds legitimately. Meanwhile the attacker in the middle collects the session cookie and walks away with an authenticated session. A caller posing as IT support can get a similar result by reading the number aloud.

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) says the same thing in one line of its ranking of MFA methods: push with number matching is resistant to push bombing and vulnerable to phishing. Phishing kits that proxy a genuine sign-in page rather than imitate one are no longer bespoke work; that is analysis, not a claim about volume. Against that technique, neither number matching nor a longer password is a defense, and neither is awareness training that teaches people to hunt for misspelled domains. The user did everything correctly and still lost the session.

Check your exceptions as well. The Network Policy Server extension, which some companies still use to add MFA to a VPN or a Remote Desktop gateway, can present plain Approve and Deny depending on its version and configuration. If that is how your remote access works, the original attack may still be open there.

The authentication paths that number matching never touches

Four routes deserve a line on an executive risk register.

  • Session and token theft. A stolen cookie replays as an already-authenticated user. No prompt, no number, no fatigue to exploit. The countermeasures live in endpoint protection, device requirements, sign-in frequency and token binding. Binding has limits today: Microsoft's Token Protection covers native apps on Windows and Apple platforms, while browser support is a preview limited to web apps that access Azure Resource Manager.
  • Consent phishing. A user is asked to grant an application permission to read mail or files. The grant is legitimate OAuth, issued by a legitimately authenticated person. The FBI's September 2026 notice is plain that changing the password does not revoke the app's token; access continues until somebody goes looking for it.
  • Device code and cross-device flows. Sign-in flows built for televisions and conference room displays make effective lures, because the victim is asked to enter a code on a genuine Microsoft page. In May 2026 the FBI described a phishing-as-a-service kit built on this flow and advised blocking it by policy, with documented exceptions. Everything looks correct because everything is correct.
  • Machine and legacy identities. Service accounts, API keys, and app registrations that were never in scope for MFA to begin with. Ask for the current list and the date it was last reviewed. The pause before the answer is informative.

None of this is exotic. All of it shares one property: the push prompt that number matching hardened is either absent or answered correctly by someone who has been deceived about what it approves.

Enrollment is now your highest-privilege operation

Here is the exposure that belongs in front of a board, and the one that tends to be reasoned about least clearly.

Every strong authentication system needs a recovery path. Phones get lost. Employees swap hardware. Someone flies to a conference and leaves the authenticator in a drawer in Sherman Oaks. That recovery path, re-enrolling an MFA method, is functionally the ability to mint a new credential for an account. It carries more power than a password reset, and it is often protected by less process.

Attackers understand the arithmetic. The call comes in late on a Friday from someone who sounds exactly like the controller, who is boarding a flight, who cannot reach email, who needs the authenticator reset right now. CISA and the FBI have documented callers persuading help desks to transfer MFA to devices the caller controls. Synthetic voice has moved from research demonstration to ordinary tooling, and a usable sample can come from a recorded webinar or a voicemail greeting.

The defense here is procedural rather than technical. Enrollment and reset requests should be verified out-of-band against something a caller cannot talk their way past: a manager approval, a callback to a number already on file, an in-person check for privileged accounts. A face on a video call is no longer in that category. Whoever answers your help desk is holding the last line of your identity policy. If nobody has written down what that person is supposed to do when the voice on the phone is urgent and familiar, that page is the first deliverable.

The decisions worth making this quarter

Number matching answered the loophole CISA was warning about in 2022. The 2026 decision is a different one, and it is as much a budgeting question as a security question.

Move the accounts that matter to phishing-resistant credentials. Passkeys and FIDO2 security keys are bound cryptographically to the domain they were registered with, so a proxy sign-in page cannot use them; the credential simply refuses to operate on the wrong domain. That is a property of the protocol, not a vendor promise. Begin with executives, finance, IT administrators, and anyone who can move money or change permissions.

Pair identity with device next. When IT security policy requires a compliant, managed device before granting access to Microsoft 365, a sign-in relayed through an attacker's machine fails. The check happens when tokens are issued and refreshed, so a token copied from a compliant device is a separate problem, which is why revocation and binding sit beside it. Conditional Access needs Entra ID P1 licensing; find out whether your plan already includes it before buying anything. In my assessment it remains the most underused control in mid-market tenants.

Three more items deserve a calendar entry: an inventory of OAuth application consents, a written list of who may approve an MFA reset, and a verification script the service desk actually follows. None of it is glamorous, and most of it is process work.

The prompt was never the control

Executives tend to hear "we have MFA" as a finished project. Authentication behaves more like a portfolio that attackers continuously reprice. Number matching was a real win, and its reward was a shift in attacker attention toward tokens, consents, machine identities, and the person who answers the phone when a caller says they have lost their device.

Organizations that handle the next few years well will be the ones that stopped asking whether MFA is turned on and started asking which of their authentication paths still rests on a human judgment call made under pressure.
