The Cloud Your Machines Signed You Up For

Los Angeles manufacturers never approved a cloud strategy. They approved machines, and each one arrived connected.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
October 9, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article

  2. Your production telemetry has an owner, and the contract decides who
  3. The vendor's service technician is a user on your network
  4. Latency is the wrong objection; dependency is the right one

Ask a manufacturing CEO in Vernon or Santa Fe Springs when the company moved to the cloud, and the answer is likely to be a date tied to accounting or email. Ask the maintenance lead the same question and the answer stretches across a decade, machine by machine, as each new press brake, coordinate measuring machine, and controller-driven cell arrived on the floor with a vendor connection already configured and a support tunnel already enabled.

Both people are describing the same company. Only one of them is describing what actually happened.

Deliberate cloud adoption by Los Angeles manufacturers is real, and where it is well run it pays: remote diagnostics can shorten downtime, quality data gets analyzed instead of filed, and scheduling stops living in one person's spreadsheet. The part that deserves an executive's attention is different. A second migration happened underneath the first one, arriving through procurement rather than IT, and it was never governed as a single program because it never looked like one. This article is for the plant leadership team that signs for the equipment.

The cloud arrived through the loading dock, not the IT budget

Capital equipment stopped being a closed system some years ago. A modern machine tool often ships with telemetry, a vendor service portal, firmware delivered over the network, and a maintenance agreement whose value depends on the manufacturer being able to see the machine. Buy the equipment and you have, in practical terms, bought a cloud service with a steel enclosure around it.

That is an observation, not a complaint. A connected machine is often better than a disconnected one. Predictive maintenance can catch a failure before it stops the line. Remote troubleshooting can spare a technician a flight from the Midwest. A plant manager who can see utilization across three shifts makes better decisions than one who cannot.

What gets missed is that each of those purchases was evaluated on throughput, tolerance, lead time, and price. None was evaluated as an IT integration, because at the moment of purchase it was not one. It became one the day the machine was commissioned and someone asked the network technician to open a port.

Across a shop floor built up over fifteen years, that produces something nobody designed: outbound connections to a dozen different vendor clouds, inbound support paths of varying age, and no single person who can list them all. Serious cloud services planning in a manufacturing environment starts with that inventory, not with a migration plan. Guidance from the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) for owners of operational technology (OT) puts it bluntly: without an inventory, organizations do not know what they have and what should be secured.

Your production telemetry has an owner, and the contract decides who

Cycle times, tool wear curves, scrap rates, oven profiles, and yield by operator are among the most commercially sensitive numbers a manufacturer produces. They describe how the company actually makes money. They are also, increasingly, flowing continuously to the firm that sold you the machine, under terms agreed to in a purchase order that finance reviewed for payment schedule and warranty period.

The useful question is whether anyone in your company can state what the agreement permits (aggregation, benchmarking against other customers, use of your operational data to train models, retention after the maintenance contract lapses), and whether you would have negotiated differently had that clause been read as a data strategy decision rather than boilerplate. That is not a suspicion about vendors. The clause decides what happens to the data either way, and counsel should read it before the next order.

There is a second reason this matters here. If any portion of your work feeds aerospace or defense supply chains, security requirements arrive through the contract, flowed down from the prime, and the details are moving. The Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) rule phases in assessments, with third-party certification planned for its second phase. In July 2026 the Pentagon suspended that phase for review. According to the CMMC accreditation body, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) standard SP 800-171 and the safeguarding clause in defense contracts (DFARS 7012) remain in place and unchanged for nearly all contractors. Check the current position with your prime and counsel before planning around it. Analysis, not prediction: a plant that cannot say where its production data goes is poorly positioned to answer a customer's security questionnaire, whichever phase is in force.

The vendor's service technician is a user on your network

Much of the security investment of the past several years went into identity. Multifactor authentication, Conditional Access, device compliance, scoped administrative rights: the perimeter stopped being the building and became the login. That work happened in the Microsoft tenant and on the laptops.

The shop floor often missed it.

Worth testing for in your own plant, because these patterns appear wherever connected equipment accumulated faster than policy: a standing remote-access account for an equipment vendor that has not been reviewed since commissioning, shared credentials on an operator panel because operators change shift, a machine-monitoring gateway authenticating with an API key stored in plain text, and a flat network where the inspection station can reach the domain controller. Each was a reasonable decision in isolation. Together they describe an access path that bypasses every control the business paid for upstairs.

CISA's short list of primary mitigations for operational technology reads like an answer to that paragraph: take OT connections off the public internet, change default passwords, secure remote access, segment IT from OT networks, and keep the ability to run manually. Modern cybersecurity work in a manufacturing setting is therefore largely identity work and segmentation work, done within limits NIST spells out: OT has performance, reliability and safety requirements of its own. Know which human and non-human identities can reach production systems. Require strong authentication for third parties. Scope access to a machine rather than a network, and put an expiration date on everything. The same test that works for any vendor works here: what could a compromised credential at that supplier reach?

Latency is the wrong objection; dependency is the right one

The reflexive manufacturing argument against cloud systems is latency: the line cannot wait on the internet. For closed-loop motion control, that is correct. For everything above the control layer, it has become a stale objection used to avoid a harder conversation.

Dependency is the harder conversation. If the scheduling platform is unreachable for a day, can the plant still release work? If a vendor portal is down during a changeover, can anyone load the correct program? When part programs, fixture offsets, and machine configurations live only on the machine itself and in the supplier's cloud, no copy exists under your control, and a controller failure becomes a re-engineering project rather than a restore.

That is a continuity question, and it belongs in the same review as fire suppression and single-source raw material. Immutable, tested disaster recovery coverage for a manufacturing environment has to extend past the file server to the artifacts that define how parts get made. Many backup designs still stop at the office.

Questions to settle before the next purchase order

The cheapest moment to govern a connected machine is before it is bought. Attach these to the order, and have the same person who signs for tolerance sign for the answers.

  • The vendor lists every outbound destination and every inbound support path, with the reason for each.
  • Support access uses named individual accounts with strong authentication, enabled per session and logged.
  • The agreement states who owns operating data and what the vendor may do with it, during and after the maintenance term.
  • The machine runs, and can be programmed, with the vendor's cloud unreachable.
  • Programs, offsets, recipes and configurations export to storage you control, on a schedule.
  • The network segment, and what the machine may reach from it, is decided before commissioning day.
  • An asset record names the owner, the firmware support period and the next access review.

Three answers to have before a customer asks for them

None of this calls for a cloud strategy document. It calls for three answers, and in our judgment a competent plant leadership team can produce them inside a month without outside help.

  • Which systems on our floor communicate outside the building, and to whom?
  • Who outside this company can reach our equipment without calling first, and how do they authenticate?
  • If our two most important platforms were unavailable for forty-eight hours, what ships and what stops?

Those are not technical trivia. They can affect eligibility with your largest customers, your answers on a cyber insurance application, and how long a bad week lasts. Our view is that the firms modernizing well in this region are the ones that can describe their own dependencies accurately, whatever their level of cloud adoption. That description is worth more than most platforms you could buy this year.
