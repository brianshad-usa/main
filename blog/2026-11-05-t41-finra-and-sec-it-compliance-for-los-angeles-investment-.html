When the Record Stops Being Email

By Brian Shad  ·  Pro Link Systems  ·  November 05, 2026

Ask the compliance officer at a Los Angeles advisory firm where the books and records live, and the answer arrives without hesitation: the email archive, the CRM, the order management system, the shared drive, the imaged client files. Then ask where the AI meeting assistant keeps its transcripts, who can retrieve them, and whether they fall under the firm's retention schedule. The pace of the conversation changes.

That pause is the compliance story of this year for broker-dealers and registered investment advisers. Not a new rule. A new place for old rules to land.

The off-channel cases were never about text messages

Regulators spent recent years pursuing firms whose people conducted business on personal devices and consumer messaging apps. The theory was never that texting is inherently dangerous. It was structural: a firm cannot supervise a channel it does not capture, and it cannot produce a record it never retained. Supervision and recordkeeping obligations assume the firm controls the channel. When the channel sits outside the firm, the obligation does not move. It simply goes unmet.

This is analysis rather than prediction, and the reasoning is plain. AI assistants reproduce that same structure across a wider surface. A notetaker joins a client call and produces a transcript and a summary. A research assistant drafts an investment rationale. An assistant inside the mailbox composes a client email that a human sends after a glance. Each one is a record about a client, created inside the business. Whether any of them land somewhere the compliance team can search is a question most firms have not asked out loud.

One difference from the messaging-app era matters more than the rest. Everyone knew what WhatsApp was; the earlier problem was at least visible. A summary written into a calendar invite by a tool a department head enabled during a free trial stays invisible until someone goes looking, and nobody goes looking until an examination request arrives.

Your vendor list is now part of your incident response plan

Regulation S-P, the SEC rule governing how firms safeguard customer information, has been strengthened to put real weight on written incident response and on the oversight of service providers that hold or can reach customer data. Confirm the current text and your own applicable compliance dates with counsel. This essay is not legal advice, and the specifics vary by firm type and size.

Set the legal mechanics aside and look at the governance consequence, which is durable regardless of how the text reads. The direction of travel assumes a firm can answer two questions quickly: which third parties hold or can reach customer information, and how fast you learn when one of them is breached. Try answering both from memory. Everyone can name the custodian and the portfolio accounting platform. The marketing automation tool connected to the CRM, the e-signature vendor, the scheduling app with calendar and contact scope, the transcription service holding recording permissions — those are rarely inventoried, and their contracts rarely contain a notification clause tight enough for the firm to meet its own deadline.

A notification obligation missed because a vendor told you late is still your failure. Third-party risk is a contracting problem before it becomes a cybersecurity problem, and the contracting happens in rooms where no technologist is usually present.

Identity controls produce evidence; most controls produce assertions

Hold onto this distinction, because it explains where budget should go. A firewall and an endpoint agent largely produce assertions — you state they are configured correctly and a reviewer takes a sample. Identity produces evidence. Conditional Access policies, sign-in logs, privileged role assignments, and session controls all generate records that can be exported, timestamped, and read by someone who was not in the room when the decision was made.

That asymmetry is why identity now sits at the center of examinations and cyber insurance underwriting alike. The questions are specific and answerable. Is multifactor authentication enforced for every user, executives and contractors included. Phishing-resistant for anyone who can move money or change client bank details. Legacy authentication protocols blocked. Access ended how quickly after a departure, and measured how. Standing administrative rights in the tenant held by whom, and reviewed when.

Firms running on Microsoft 365 frequently own these capabilities already and have never configured them. Distance between owning a control and operating a control is where findings live. In our opinion it is the highest-return remediation available to a forty-person advisory firm this year, because a single body of work improves the security posture and the examination posture at the same time.

Retention was built for the mailbox

Retention architecture at most small and mid-sized securities firms was designed around email. Messages flow into an archive, the archive is searchable, supervision runs lexicon review against it. That arrangement held for two decades while the real business conversation migrated into chat threads, channel posts, documents with comment histories, recorded video calls, and now machine-generated summaries of all of the above.

Copilot-style assistants add a second complication that gets misread constantly. The tool does not break permissions. It honors them. If a folder of compensation data or a client file was overshared to a broad internal group years ago, an assistant will surface it instantly to anyone entitled to ask. AI did not create that exposure. It removed the obscurity that was quietly doing the work of access control.

Retention and recoverability are related but separate obligations, and firms conflate them constantly. Preserving a record for supervision is not the same as restoring an environment after ransomware, which is why immutable data backup and a rehearsed recovery sequence belong in the same conversation as the retention schedule rather than in a different budget line entirely.

What to decide this quarter

The decision in front of you is not whether to adopt AI. Your staff already made that decision informally, one trial signup at a time. What remains open is whether the firm defines the sanctioned set before someone else asks what the unsanctioned set contains.

Three concrete moves, in order:

  • Inventory the surfaces. Every place client information can be recorded, transcribed, or summarized, including browser extensions and anything connected to your tenant through an OAuth grant. Most of the surprises live in that last category.
  • Name the sanctioned tools and give people somewhere to go. A short approved list with retention and supervision wired in beats a policy document nobody reads. A responsive help desk is also the best shadow-AI detector a firm has, because people mention the tool they are using when they call about something else. Ban nothing you have not replaced, or the usage simply goes deeper.
  • Rewrite the vendor questions. Notification timelines, data location, subprocessor disclosure, and model training terms belong in the contract, not in a sales deck.

None of this is exotic. It is ordinary governance applied to tools that arrived faster than the governance did, which is the recurring condition of running a regulated business while the software changes quarterly. The firms that handle the next few years well will not be the ones with the strictest AI policy. They will be the ones who can answer, on the day they are asked, where the record is.
