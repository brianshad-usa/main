Break-Glass Access Nobody Tested

Emergency admin access fails quietly, and passwordless removed the fallback. Why identity lockout is now a board-level decision, not an IT hygiene checkbox.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
September 15, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
7 min read
In this article
  1. The lockout you cause yourself is the likelier one
  2. Passwordless removed a fallback nobody wrote down
  3. Outage and takeover look identical at 3 a.m.
  4. The runbook lives behind the door you are trying to open
  5. Three questions before the next configuration change

A Conditional Access policy is edited on a Tuesday morning to require compliant devices. It is scoped to All users, and the exclusion list is empty. Twenty minutes later sign-ins begin failing across the company. Among the accounts that can no longer authenticate are the two administrators who could turn the policy off.

That is not a prediction about your business. It is a description of a mechanism, and the mechanism exists wherever sign-in rules are enforced from one place. Microsoft's own instructions for that policy say to exclude emergency access accounts and begin in report-only mode, which tells you how easily the alternative happens. No adversary is required. One checkbox and an ordinary morning will do it. This essay is for the officers who decide who may take control of the company's systems in an emergency, and the IT leaders who have to make that possible. The decision it informs is how emergency access is built, who holds it, and when it was last proven to work.

The lockout you cause yourself is the likelier one

Break-glass accounts entered the IT vocabulary when identity was a convenience layer. Email had its own password. The file server had its own password. Losing access to one system was an inconvenience rather than an event.

Consolidation changed the shape of that risk. Single sign-on is now the front door to mail, documents, finance, the CRM, the VPN, the ticketing system, the security console, and often the phone system. That is genuinely good for IT security: one place to enforce multifactor authentication, one place to cut off a departing employee, one audit trail. It also means the blast radius of an identity failure is the whole company rather than one application.

Judgment, not statistics: the most probable cause of that failure is internal. Policy scope applied too broadly. A rule that assumes a device state the emergency account can never satisfy. A federation certificate that expired because the calendar reminder belonged to someone who left. An authentication method disabled across the tenant while an administrator still depended on it. None of these are attacks. They are configuration, and configuration changes every week.

Passwordless removed a fallback nobody wrote down

For years the unstated recovery plan in many businesses was a password. It was an assumption rather than a documented plan. Somewhere there was a long string on a card in a drawer, and if everything else failed, someone would type it.

That plan has expired. Microsoft now requires multifactor authentication to sign in to the Azure, Entra, Intune and Microsoft 365 admin centers, with no opt-out, and break-glass accounts are included. A password on a card no longer opens the door it was kept for.

Passkeys and hardware-backed authentication are the right direction. Moving away from shared secrets improves protection and user experience at once. It also changes what can be held in reserve. A passkey bound to an administrator's own phone cannot be put in a safe. A hardware security key can. If the service that registers new factors is the unavailable thing, there is no path to add one. If the phone holding the authenticator is the phone that was lost, the fallback and the primary share a failure mode.

The design rule that follows is an engineering judgment rather than a standard: build emergency access so that no two recovery paths share a dependency. In practice that means more than one break-glass identity, each surviving a different kind of failure. Microsoft's guidance on emergency access accounts points the same way, and its main requirements make a usable checklist:

  • At least two emergency accounts, cloud-only, on the tenant's onmicrosoft.com domain, neither federated nor synchronized, and not tied to one individual.
  • Global Administrator assigned as permanently active, so that no approval step stands in the way.
  • A passkey on a hardware security key, or certificate-based authentication, and a different method from the one everyday administrators use.
  • Exclusion from every Conditional Access policy that blocks or restricts sign-in, and from any automated cleanup of unused accounts.
  • Credentials kept in separate, secure, fireproof places that more than one authorized person can reach.
  • An alert on every sign-in, and a tested sign-in at least every 90 days and after any change in IT staff.

Excluding an account from policy makes security teams uncomfortable, as it should. The strong credential and the alarm are what pay for it. The alternative is an emergency account that is perfectly secured and completely unusable.

Outage and takeover look identical at 3 a.m.

Executives hear "break-glass" and picture an outage. The provider has a bad day, service degrades, everyone waits. That is annoying, expensive in lost hours, and largely survivable with communication discipline and an out-of-band way to reach staff.

Contested control is the harder case, and the reasoning is structural rather than sourced from any incident report. In a consolidated tenant, identity is the most valuable thing an intruder can hold, because holding it makes every other control negotiable: authentication methods can be added, sessions for legitimate administrators can be revoked, trust relationships can be altered. The identity platform is perfectly healthy in that scenario. It is simply working for someone else.

Both emergencies sound the same on the first phone call. They demand opposite responses. One calls for patience. The other calls for immediate isolation, and the credentials needed for isolation are worthless if they live in a password vault that authenticates through the platform now under someone else's control. Emergency credentials belong outside the system they recover: physically, ideally split between two people, with the tamper seal serving as the audit record.

One newer wrinkle deserves naming. In many companies the practical break-glass path has been "call IT and get a reset." The FBI warns that criminals use AI-generated audio to impersonate people, and real-time video of supposed executives, which means recognition is no longer proof of anything. If your emergency procedure depends on someone knowing a voice, or on a video call as identity evidence, the procedure has a hole in it. Deciding in advance who may authorize emergency access, and what counts as proof when a face and a voice no longer do, is part of the design.

The runbook lives behind the door you are trying to open

Draw the dependency map once and it is difficult to unsee. Where does the incident runbook live? If the answer is SharePoint, and SharePoint authenticates through the platform that is down, the runbook does not exist during the one hour it matters. Ask the same question of the backup console, the endpoint detection portal, the documentation wiki, the vendor contact list, and the chat tool you would use to coordinate. Plenty of organizations have rehearsed disaster recovery for servers and data and have never once run the exercise for identity, which in our judgment is now the more probable failure.

Then there is rot. A credential nobody has used may not work. Expiration policies quietly invalidate it. Tenant changes made since it was created were never applied to it. The safe combination is known by someone who left two years ago. An untested emergency account is a belief rather than a control. Exercising it on a schedule, with two people present and the result written down, converts belief into fact, and when the exercise surfaces something broken, that is the point.

This is also the moment to settle an ownership question that matters more than it sounds. If an outside provider administers your Microsoft 365 tenant under a managed IT services agreement, whose break-glass credentials are they? Microsoft's delegated access for partners is granular, time-bound and granted by the customer, which suits daily administration and is no substitute for the customer's own way in. An arrangement where the only emergency access belongs to a vendor is a single point of failure with a contract wrapped around it. The healthier structure is explicit: the client holds sovereign access to its own identity platform, the provider holds operational access, and both are documented.

Three questions before the next configuration change

Break-glass access is the technology equivalent of check-signing authority: a decision about who can unilaterally take control of the company, under what conditions, and with which witnesses. That belongs on the officer agenda rather than in a configuration ticket.

  • Which recovery tools depend on the system they are meant to recover?
  • When was emergency access last used successfully, by whom, and who watched?
  • If someone called tonight claiming to be a locked-out executive, what would prove it?

The third question is why the human path matters as much as the technical one. Whoever answers the help desk phone is part of the control. Know who picks up, and know what they will ask you to prove.
