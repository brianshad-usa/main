Cybersecurity Best Practices for Pasadena

A short sequence of controls for Pasadena technology and healthcare organizations, with the evidence to ask for at each step.

Pro Link SystemsInsights
July 31, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026
4 min read
In this article
  1. Where to start: a sequence for the first quarter
  2. Healthcare organizations: what the rules ask in general terms
  3. Technology companies: customers will ask
  4. How to judge whether it is working

Cybersecurity best practices for Pasadena technology and healthcare organizations come down to a short list done in the right order, and to proof that each item works. A company near Old Pasadena that builds software and a medical practice serving the San Gabriel Valley hold different data, but both are judged on the same few controls: who can sign in, whether they can recover, and how quickly they notice a problem. This article is for owners, chief executives and practice administrators who need to decide what to fund first and how to check that it was done. The regulatory points are an overview; confirm them with qualified counsel.

Where to start: a sequence for the first quarter

  1. Lock down sign-in. Require multi-factor authentication on email, remote access, billing and any system holding customer or patient data. The CISA ransomware guide recommends phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication for email, VPNs and accounts that reach critical systems. Escalate any system that cannot support it.
  2. Make recovery real. Keep an offline, encrypted copy of critical data and test restores on a schedule, as the same guide advises. The common 3-2-1 pattern (three copies, two media types, one offsite) is a reasonable design target. Record how long a full restore actually took; that number is your recovery time, whatever the plan says. See backup and disaster recovery.
  3. Keep devices and software current. Centrally managed endpoint protection and a patch cycle with an owner. Ask for a monthly report of devices missing updates, not an assurance that updates are applied.
  4. Filter email and train people on it. Email filtering reduces what arrives; short, frequent training and an easy way to report suspicious messages handle the rest. Measure the share of staff who report a test phish, and make reporting free of blame.
  5. Write the incident plan and rehearse it. Name who decides to isolate systems, who calls counsel and the insurer, and who speaks to customers or patients. The guide asks that the plan be approved in writing by the chief executive or equivalent and kept offline as well as online.
  6. Review vendors with access. List every outside party that can reach your systems or data, and what each could do with that access.

Healthcare organizations: what the rules ask in general terms

Medical practices, labs and other covered entities, and their business associates, are subject to the HIPAA Security Rule. In general terms it expects a documented risk analysis, safeguards that fit the organization's size and complexity, and written agreements with vendors that handle patient information. NIST's SP 800-66 Revision 2 is a free resource guide that maps the rule to practical security activities and is written for regulated entities of all sizes. HHS has proposed changes to the Security Rule; ask counsel whether any have taken effect, since the current rule remains in force until that happens.

Practical decisions: who owns the risk analysis and when it was last updated; whether every vendor that stores or transmits patient data has a signed business associate agreement; and whether system access logs are reviewed by a person, not only retained. Whether your arrangements satisfy the rule is a question for counsel or a qualified assessor. The page on healthcare IT covers the operational side.

Technology companies: customers will ask

Software and device companies in Pasadena are more likely to meet security rules through their customers than through a regulator. Enterprise buyers often send security questionnaires and may ask for an independent attestation such as a SOC 2 report, which a certified public accountant issues after examining a service organization's controls (see the AICPA overview). Treat a questionnaire as a preview of what you will be asked to prove. Keep source code, signing keys and cloud administrator accounts under the strictest controls, and separate production from development access. Cloud services accounts deserve the same multi-factor and review discipline as email.

How to judge whether it is working

Ask your IT provider or internal lead for five facts each quarter: the share of accounts protected by multi-factor authentication, the date and duration of the last restore test, the number of devices missing critical updates, the number of phishing reports from staff, and the date of the last incident-plan rehearsal. If those cannot be produced, the controls cannot be verified. A help desk that can answer them quickly is usually a sign the data is being kept, and a cybersecurity review by someone outside the team that runs the controls will test the answers.
