Business Process Automation for LA Companies

Automate stable, high-volume work first, and treat every automated workflow as a system with an owner, limited access and a fallback.

Business process automation: where it pays and where it does not

Business process automation (BPA) means using software to carry out repeatable steps of a workflow, such as routing an invoice for approval or creating an account when someone is hired, without a person moving the data by hand. This article is for owners and operations or finance leaders at Los Angeles companies, from Beverly Hills professional firms to Valley distributors, who are deciding which processes to automate first and what oversight the automation needs. The short version: automate stable, high-volume, rule-based work, and treat each automation as a system that needs an owner, access controls and a way to stop it.

Choose processes that are stable, frequent and rule-based

Automation rewards processes that run often, follow clear rules and have a known correct outcome. It punishes processes that are still changing or depend on judgement. Typical candidates in a small or midsize company:

  • Approvals. Purchase requests, expense reports and time-off requests that currently travel by email.
  • Onboarding and offboarding. Creating accounts, assigning licenses, and, just as important, removing access on the last day.
  • Intake and routing. Web-form or email inquiries that need to reach the right person, with a record of who handled them.
  • Document handling. Moving files to the right folder, requesting signatures, and reminding people of deadlines.
  • Reporting. Pulling the same figures from the same systems on a schedule.

Avoid starting with a process that nobody has written down, one that has many exceptions, or one that touches regulated records unless someone qualified has reviewed the data handling. For businesses in regulated fields such as healthcare, involve your compliance adviser or counsel before automating anything that handles sensitive records, and see how healthcare IT is typically set up.

What the technology usually consists of

Companies already on Microsoft 365 often have a starting point in Power Automate, which Microsoft describes as a service for building automated workflows between apps and services to synchronize files, send notifications and collect data. Other platforms exist, and the choice matters less than three questions: where the data lives, who can build and change the workflows, and whether the tool can connect to the systems you already use. Systems connect through application programming interfaces (APIs), which is why a vendor's list of supported connections deserves a careful read. Work in cloud services is easiest to automate because the connections are already designed to be used by software.

Artificial intelligence can read documents and draft responses, but it makes mistakes. Keep a person in the approval step wherever an error would cost money, affect a customer or touch a legal record.

Automation needs governance, not just a builder

The most common failure is a well-meaning employee building a workflow that works, then leaves, and nobody knows it exists. Two features of Power Automate show why oversight matters. Every member of an organization can access the default environment, where flows are created unless an administrator sets up separate ones, and flows built there can handle company data. Microsoft's data policies let administrators limit which connectors a workflow may use, as guardrails against unintentionally exposing organizational data. Whatever platform you choose, ask for the equivalent:

  • A named owner and a backup owner for every automated workflow
  • A register of what each workflow does, which systems it touches and which account it runs under
  • Service accounts with only the access the workflow needs, protected by multi-factor authentication where the platform supports it
  • Rules about which external services workflows may send company data to
  • An alert when a workflow fails, and a documented manual fallback
  • Inclusion of the workflow platform in backup and recovery planning, and review with your cybersecurity provider

A sequence for the first project

  1. Map the process. Write down every step, the people involved, how long it takes today and how often it goes wrong. Walk through it with the people who do the work.
  2. Estimate the value honestly. Multiply hours saved by loaded cost, then subtract build, license and maintenance effort. Also count the less visible gains, such as fewer errors and quicker responses, but keep them separate.
  3. Fix the process first. Remove steps that exist only out of habit. Automating a confusing process only makes confusion faster.
  4. Start small and run in parallel. Automate one process, run it alongside the manual version for a few cycles, and compare results before switching over.
  5. Train and communicate. Tell staff what changes, what stays manual and who to call when it fails.
  6. Review quarterly. Check error rates, time saved against the estimate and whether the process still matches how the business works. Retire workflows that no longer earn their upkeep.

How to judge success

Pick two or three measures before you start: cycle time from request to completion, error or rework rate, and hours of manual effort per month. Compare them after one quarter. If the savings are smaller than estimated, find out whether the estimate was wrong or the automation was incomplete, then decide whether to extend or stop. Smaller, measured projects teach more than a large program launched on a promised percentage saving. A help desk or IT partner can build and support the workflows, but the business owns the process and should own the decision to automate it. For companies in places such as Century City that run lean back offices, one or two well-chosen workflows are usually enough to start, and managed IT services can cover the support side.
